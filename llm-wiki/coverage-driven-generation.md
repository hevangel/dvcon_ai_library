# Coverage-Driven Generation

> *"Constrained-random verification gets you to 80% coverage almost for free. The next 15% costs you a week of constraint tuning. The last 5% costs you months, and if you ask the field, that's where projects actually die. Coverage-driven generation is the discipline — and increasingly the ML pipeline — that closes the gap by generating stimulus where the coverage is missing, rather than just running more random seeds and hoping. The pattern is so universal it has its own acronym salad: CDG, CDCRV, coverage-directed test generation. The honest version is a feedback loop: a coverage database tells you which bins are empty, something decides what stimulus would fill them, the something emits new constraints or new tests, the simulator runs them, and the loop repeats until you either hit 100% or give up and file a waiver. So how does that 'something' actually work, and why are we now training neural nets and reinforcement-learning agents to be it? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**Coverage-driven generation** (CDG) is the closing loop layered on top of constrained-random verification. Functional coverage is "the main metric for measuring stimulus quality in a metric driven verification (MDV)," and engineers use it "to sign off the complex design verification features" [Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure, 2019]. The problem Kodi et al. lay out is mechanical but brutal: "to get coverage closure, large numbers of simulations are required. It is then necessary to merge the coverage data from those simulations which can take a significant amount of time and effort, only to realize that total coverage is still not 100%. This becomes an iterative process where coverage is analyzed, constraints updated, and tests rerun till the target is achieved" [Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure, 2019]. CDG is anything that automates one turn of that iteration.

The conceptual core is a **feedback loop from coverage back to stimulus**. Salah names it explicitly: "we need an automated technique to automate the feedback from coverage report analysis to test generation process," and proposes a genetic algorithm that "accelerates coverage-driven functional verification … by covering uncovered scenarios in the coverage report (coverage holes)" [New Trends in RTL Verification, 2015]. Bansal's framing is sharper still: "constrained random simulations … do[es] not ensure with complete certainty meeting the coverage goals, even after running multiple random seeds or increasing volume of data traffic," so he writes "goal driven tests by use of smart constraint modelling, governed by feedback" [Goal Driven Stimulus Solution, 2021]. CDG, in other words, replaces brute-force seed multiplication with intent.

## How it's used in practice

The practical implementations cluster into three families. The first is **constraint rewriting from coverage**, classic CDG. Wang and Pusphaparaj describe the manual ancestor — "override the constraints, rerun the tests and merge again to get a complete report" [Practical experience in automatic functional coverage convergence …, 2014] — and Kodi et al. automate exactly this into a "fully automated functional coverage closure method" that maps each bin to its own coverpoint and lets a script iterate [Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure, 2019]. The second family is **blackbox optimization over test parameters**: Huang et al.'s Smart Regression Planner formulates "test parameter configuration as a blackbox optimization problem" and "drives the parameters update with a Bayesian optimization technique that uses coverage from nightly regressions as feedback" [Test Parameter Tuning with Blackbox Optimization, 2022]. Roy et al. push the same Bayesian + GBRT idea into "live online flows" that retune constraints during a single simulation [Dynamically Optimized Test Generation Using Machine Learning, 2021].

The third family, and the dominant one in the 2024-2026 corpus, is **learned stimulus generation**. Shah et al.'s AFCML uses K-means++ clustering to "pinpoint the most relevant stimuli from a broad set," reporting "improvements in transaction efficiency by 49 percent" [AFCML, 2025]. Pallela et al. close 3D NAND Flash coverage with a reinforcement-learning agent whose "feedback loop is established to train the model from the coverage database to the RL agent and from the RL agent to stimulus generation," coupled with gradient ascent "to determine desired stimulus settings that cover the highest number of unit coverage bins" [Stimulus Diversification …, 2025]. Kumar et al. tackle the million-bin SoC interconnect problem with "intelligent stimulus generation" [AI-accelerating coverage closure …, 2025], and Baek et al. fold heatmap visualization of the gaps together with ML test selection to "reduce regression testing time" by 63% while pushing SDC coverage to 100% on timing-exception paths [Enhancing SDC Verification in SoCs, 2025]. The loop is the same in all of them; the learned policy is what changed.

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The standing pitfall is that the loop is only as good as the coverage model underneath it. Kodi et al. note that "even using methodologies such as the UVM, and the built-in System Verilog features for functional coverage, implementation can be inconsistent and error-prone," and that "behind every coverage definition there is a cost" — designing, collecting, and analyzing coverage is itself labor [Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure, 2019]. Bansal warns of a subtler failure mode: "random stability concerns for derivative projects" when goal-driven tests and their seeds are inherited across tapeouts [Goal Driven Stimulus Solution, 2021]. And Dahir et al. remind us that the cheapest lever is sometimes upstream: their Design-for-Verification work with HLS improves the *verifiability* of multi-pipeline designs with resource sharing, so CDG has less ground to cover in the first place [Using HLS to improve Design-for-Verification …, 2021].

Where the field is heading is unambiguously toward **autonomous closed-loop verification**. Noh et al. frame the whole problem as a control system: "the goal is defined by sign-off objectives … the state is the current observed condition of the regression … the feedback is the stream of results returned by each run," and they build "an AI-driven closed-loop regression architecture in which each run's outcomes can be fed back into the next" [Multi-Agent Orchestration for Autonomous Regression Management, 2026]. Sharma et al.'s "Al/ML-driven regression optimization methodology" uses "historical regression data to predict test utility, dynamically prioritize execution, and continuously adapt to evolving verification goals" [Beyond Heuristics, 2026], and Chung et al.'s Smart Sanity Regression orchestrates change-impact analysis, intelligent test-set optimization, and failure triage into "a single, end-to-end sanity regression workflow" [A Novel Fast Regression, 2026]. CDG started as a script that rewrites constraints; it is converging on an agent that owns the regression. Khairallah and Ghoneima's reusable UVM processor methodology [2014] and Wang and Pusphaparaj's reusable collection infrastructure [2014] are the roots this agent tree grew from.
