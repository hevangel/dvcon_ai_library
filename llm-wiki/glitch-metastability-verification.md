# Glitch and Metastability Verification

> *"Here is a fact that keeps every SoC team awake: clock domain crossing (CDC) issues are the second most common reason for silicon re-spins [A Systematic Take on Addressing Dynamic CDC Verification Challenges, 2019]. And the really nasty part is that simulation is silent about the whole problem. RTL simulators do not model metastability. The setup/hold violation that wrecks a flop in real silicon simply does not happen in your testbench — the flop happily samples whatever you tell it to, on the clock edge you drew. So you can run a million seeds, hit 100% coverage, and still ship a chip that randomly drops a bit when two asynchronous clocks meet on the floor.

> Worse, the same trap extends to resets. Modern SoCs juggle a dozen reset domains — soft resets, hard resets, resets gated by power state — and 'different reset domains on transmitter and receiver does not necessarily imply that a path is unsafe, [and] the same reset domains do not necessarily imply that a path is safe' [Techniques to identify reset metastability issues due to soft resets, 2023]. The bug lives in the gap between what you assumed and what the silicon does.

> So how do you verify something your simulator will not even show you? That is what this page is about — and the answer turns out to be a mix of structural analysis, injected metastability models, formal glitch hunting, and a lot of painful lessons from teams that shipped first and learned second. Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**Metastability** is what happens when a flip-flop's input violates setup or hold time near the active clock edge. The flop's output neither cleanly settles to 0 nor 1 but hovers in a quasi-stable state for some random extra delay before resolving. In a single clock domain this is bad enough; across an asynchronous **clock domain crossing (CDC)** it is the central reliability problem of any multi-clock SoC [Verification of Clock Domain Crossing Jitter and Metastability Tolerance using Emulation, 2012]. The standard fix is a synchronizer — usually a two-flop chain — that gives the metastable signal time to resolve before downstream logic uses it. But that fix is only as good as the assumptions baked into the synchronizer, and CDC verification exists to make sure those assumptions actually hold.

CDC verification has two halves. The first is **structural analysis**: a static tool walks the RTL, finds every signal that crosses between clock domains, and classifies each crossing as having a good, bad, or missing synchronizer [Verification of Clock Domain Crossing Jitter and Metastability Tolerance using Emulation, 2012]. The second half is **dynamic CDC verification**, which checks that the synchronizer's protocol assumptions (no combinational glitches at the boundary, multi-bit signals grey-coded or handshaked, and so on) are not violated during simulation [A Systematic Take on Addressing Dynamic CDC Verification Challenges, 2019]. The same idea, applied to resets instead of clocks, gives you **reset domain crossing (RDC)** verification — and RDC is the quieter, nastier cousin because, as Mirsky pointed out back in 2017, 'there is little awareness in the industry of the dangers of RDC' [Comprehensive and Automated Static Tool Based Strategies for the Detection and Resolution of Reset Domain Crossings, 2017].

## How it is used in practice

Because simulation cannot see metastability, you have to **inject** it. Hari, Krishnamurthy, and Jain laid out the requirements back in 2012: a metastability injection model must introduce random extra delay only when (1) the data actually changes, (2) it is sampled, and (3) setup/hold is violated; it must do this independently at every CDC bit; and it must come with a debug story, because debugging functional errors with hundreds of injections active is otherwise hopeless [Verification of Clock Domain Crossing Jitter and Metastability Tolerance using Emulation, 2012]. Their flow was even *formal-friendly*, so the same model could be used in a parallel formal proof. Takara, Kwok, and Jain later productized automatically generated metastability delay models that randomly add or subtract a cycle at the RX register depending on whether the violation is setup or hold [Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification -What have we learned?, 2015].

Reset networks get the same treatment. Kaur and Khare describe an 'intelligent RDC' flow that detects asynchronous reset-assertion-induced metastability and the glitches caused by combinational logic in reset paths [Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure with Intelligent Reset Domain Crossings Detection, 2020]. Sinha and Dornala applied it to a client PCIe design where 'verifying that a design can be correctly reset under all modes of operation presents a significant challenge' given multiple reset domains and complex reset distribution [Exhaustive Reset Verification Enablement: Client PCIE Design Reset Verification Case Study, 2019]. The trick with soft resets is that the reset domain a flop nominally belongs to does not tell you whether it is safe — the soft reset logic on the transmitter and receiver is what matters [Techniques to identify reset metastability issues due to soft resets, 2023].

On the glitch side, automated formal analysis has become the weapon of choice. Hsiung, Hari, and Khare target 'the silent chip-killer — glitch defects on asynchronous paths' — short pulses that get captured when they cross a clock domain [Preventing Chip-Killing Glitches on CDC Paths with Automated Formal Analysis, 2018]. Yan, Yeung, and Li extended this with the 'Three Witches' methodology, finding that once they had gate-level CDC in hand they had to verify glitches on clock and reset paths too, not just data multiplexing paths [Preventing Glitch Nightmares on CDC Paths: The Three Witches, 2021].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The biggest pitfall is treating CDC as a one-shot, signoff-style check. Cunningham and Sobanski from Intel make the case that a real CDC methodology has to be **closed loop**: false violations get filtered and re-verified automatically, and design-rule checks 'ensure CDC logic is preserved from RTL through to the final layout tapeout netlist' [Implementation of a closed loop CDC verification methodology, 2014]. Lose that thread between RTL and netlist and your CDC signoff is meaningless — the synchronizer you proved at RTL can be optimized away by synthesis.

The second pitfall is power. Once a chip has multiple power domains, isolation cells, and retention registers, the CDC analysis has to be **power-aware**: a CDC signal that is fine when both domains are on becomes a glitch source when the transmitter's domain is being power-gated [Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification -What have we learned?, 2015]. Handover, Lovett, and Takara extend this to DVFS, where voltage and frequency scaling on the fly introduces 'CDC issues which are introduced as a result of the low power design approaches' [Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015].

The third pitfall is coverage — when does dynamic CDC signoff actually close? Each synchronizer depends on a set of protocol assumptions, and 'when violated can make the transfer unreliable'; without explicitly checking those assumptions in simulation, you have no coverage of dynamic CDC [A Systematic Take on Addressing Dynamic CDC Verification Challenges, 2019]. And on safety-critical FPGAs, Gnusin reminds us that DO-254 requires CDC to be validated on real hardware, not just simulation [FPGA-based Clock Domain Crossing Validation for Safety-Critical Designs, 2019].

The field is converging on three things: formal for structural proofs, intelligent injection for dynamic stress, and a unified CDC+RDC+power story. Kumar, Khan, and Mittra present a 'pragmatic formal' methodology that proves CDC properties on SoCs where simulation and STA are not enough [Pragmatic Formal Verification Methodology for Clock Domain Crossing (CDC), 2023]. Gulliya, Singh, and Bora tackle multi-bit transactions and bus protocols where the 2-D flip-flop trick alone does not cut it [Key Gochas in implementing CDC for various Bus Protocols, 2020]. The era of 'we ran a CDC tool and waived the rest' is ending — the cost of a respin is too high.

- **Key Gochas in implementing CDC for various Bus Protocols** (2020, DVCon US) — Nikita Gulliya, Mukesh Kumar Singh and Abhishek Bora. Spec-driven automation of CDC-correct codegen for multi-bit bus transactions.
