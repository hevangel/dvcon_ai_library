# Coverage Closure and Convergence

> *You launch 50,000 random tests overnight, the sun comes up, and coverage moved from 87% to 87.4%. The last few percent of coverage is the part that ruins schedules — it's where the cheap stimulus runs out and the design starts hiding its corner cases behind constraints the solver can't quite reach. DVCon authors have been writing about this exact pain for over a decade, and the story is the same every time: you tweak a constraint, rerun the regression, merge coverage, see the bar move a hair, and repeat. The good news is the field has moved on from pure manual elbow grease to directed tests, portable stimulus, ML-driven knob tuning, and now agentic AI flows that close the loop without you. Let's dig in.*

## What "coverage closure" actually means

**Coverage closure** is the process of driving a verification metric — usually functional coverage, often augmented by code coverage — to an agreed target, typically (but not always) 100% of the modeled bins, before tape-out. The reason it deserves its own wiki page rather than being folded into "functional coverage" is the word *closure*: the hard part isn't defining coverage, it's the last few percent. As Wang and Pusphaparaj observed back in 2014, getting functional coverage to converge "in a short time is hard," and the standard MDV loop — random tests, merge coverage, override constraints, rerun, repeat — was already visibly inefficient [Practical experience in automatic functional coverage convergence, 2014]. A decade later the diagnosis is unchanged: closing coverage on modern SoCs is "effort-intensive and time-consuming, consuming up to 20-25% of the overall verification cycle" [Unified Coverage Methodology, 2024].

The reason the tail is so expensive is partly combinatorial and partly modeling. Cross coverage — combinations of two or more coverpoints — is where closure usually stalls, because the random solver needs a very specific joint configuration to hit a particular bin, and that configuration may be many constraints deep [Reaching 100% Functional Coverage Using Machine Learning, 2025]. SoC interconnects make this worse: hundreds of masters and slaves, multiple protocols, and the legal transaction space balloons well past what brute-force random can enumerate [Functional Coverage Closure in SoC Interconnect Verification with Iterative Machine Learning, 2025]. The closure problem is, in effect, a search problem whose state space grows faster than compute.

It is also an iterative data-management problem. Every cycle of the loop re-runs simulations and re-merges coverage databases, which is itself a non-trivial cost; practical experience reports that the merge step alone can dominate the iteration time [Practical experience in automatic functional coverage convergence, 2014]. Modern tooling (e.g., Synopsys VSO.ai) attempts to remove this friction by automatically biasing the solver with a "compensated distribution" that nullifies distribution skew across both intra- and inter-simulation runs, exposing rare bugs and reducing the repeated generation of identical stimuli [Unified Coverage Methodology, 2024].

## How teams actually close coverage in practice

The toolbox falls into roughly four families, and most projects use a combination. First, **manual constraint override and directed tests**: the engineer reads the coverage report, identifies a hole, writes a directed test or adds a `constraint` block to force the relevant combination. This is the original MDV loop and it still works, but it scales poorly — Tankwal et al. describe building "Smart Coverage Closure" tooling around test ranking and Perl scripting just to make the directed-test route tractable on a complex High-Speed Link DV where "an extensive number of functional coverage bins must be addressed within strict time constraints" [A Comprehensive High Speed Link Verification Test Bench, 2024].

Second, **smarter stimulus generation**. Graph-based stimulus and auto-generated stimulus are proposed as automatable alternatives to constrained random that "take up less resources, memory, and verification time by an order of magnitude" [An Analysis of Stimulus Techniques for Efficient Functional Coverage Closure, 2021]. Portable Stimulus Standard (PSS) takes a related angle: by raising the abstraction level of stimulus, it reduces the manual intervention needed for the last 1-2% of bins [Increasing Regression Efficiency with Portable Stimulus, 2021]. The Boosting SystemC paper goes further and automatically integrates uncovered coverage bins into the stimuli model as additional constraints, enabling genuine coverage-driven generation in a SystemC flow [Boosting SystemC-based Testbenches with Modern C++ and Coverage-Driven Generation, 2015].

Third, **machine-learning-assisted closure**. The 2024-2025 wave of papers treats coverage holes as a prediction problem: train a model on past regressions, predict the configuration most likely to fill the next hole, run it, repeat. Kwon et al. apply iterative ML to SoC interconnect verification [Functional Coverage Closure in SoC Interconnect Verification with Iterative Machine Learning, 2025]; Kim et al. push cross coverage all the way to 100% with a Python-based coverage agent and careful model-accuracy strategies, explicitly targeting the scenario where "predictions continue to fail and the coverage rate either barely increases or doesn't increase at all" before eventually converging [Reaching 100% Functional Coverage Using Machine Learning, 2025].

Fourth, **fully automated closure flows** that try to remove the human from the loop entirely. Kodi et al.'s "Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure" and Lee et al.'s Python-based environment both aim to let the testbench drive its own closure, with the latter notable for showing how to reuse SystemVerilog's coverage features from inside Python [Fully Automated Functional Coverage Closure, 2019; Functional Coverage Closure with Python, 2024].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

A few recurring traps deserve calling out. Coverage that "closes" can still miss bugs: code coverage only shows that the code was *executed*, not that it was correct, and an over-constrained testbench can quietly declare bins unreachable when the real problem is the constraint itself [Unified Coverage Methodology, 2024]. Merging coverage from heterogeneous sources (simulation, formal, emulation) without understanding what each metric measures can fool you into believing you've made more progress than you have — a theme that recurs across the corpus. And the obsession with 100% can itself be a trap: some bins are unreachable by design, and identifying those requires either formal unreachability analysis or a willingness to waive with justification.

The trajectory of the field is clear from the recency of the papers. We are moving from human-driven MDV loops toward ML- and AI-driven closed loops, where a coverage agent iteratively predicts, runs, and learns. Unified methodologies that merge coverage across simulation, formal, and emulation — and apply ML across all three — are becoming the norm rather than the exception [Unified Coverage Methodology, 2024]. Whether the long-promised "click a button, get 100%" closure ever fully arrives, the amount of human toil in the tail is demonstrably shrinking.
