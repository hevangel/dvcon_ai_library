# Network-on-Chip (NoC) and Interconnect Verification

> *"In a modern AI accelerator, every core, every memory controller and every specialized IP is stitched together by a fabric that pushes terabytes per second through a grid of routers and links. That fabric is the Network-on-Chip, and it is the one piece of the chip where a one-cycle-per-hop decision compounds into a 40% throughput swing. Tushar Garg and Ranjan Mahajan hit this directly in 2025 when they built a fully customizable SystemC Transaction Level Model and found that simply moving where a Requester Node sits on a router — without touching the protocol or the clock — could cut end-to-end latency dramatically and change which buffer became the bottleneck [System design exploration with fully customizable NoC, 2025]. The verification problem is brutal precisely because a NoC is distributed, parallel and reactive: the interesting bugs are deadlocks, livelocks and starvations that only fire under specific traffic patterns, and they routinely escape pre-silicon simulation and even emulation, costing a re-spin when they finally surface on silicon [Mechanism to Generate FIFO VC Dependency Graph and Its Application to System Level Deadlock Verification, 2021]. Nvidia, sitting on some of the largest NoCs ever taped out, has spent two consecutive DVCons arguing the only way to close this is hierarchical formal. So what is actually moving through the routers, and why is it so hard to verify? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

A **Network-on-Chip** is a packet-switched communication fabric made of routers and links. Its personality is captured by four knobs: **topology** (mesh, ring, torus, crossbar or any custom shape), **routing algorithm**, **switching and flow control**, and **arbitration and buffering** — including the **virtual channels (VCs)** that let one physical link carry multiple logical traffic classes without head-of-line blocking [System design exploration with fully customizable NoC, 2025]. On top of this plumbing sit the protocols the routers actually speak: AMBA CHI for cache-coherent fabrics, with its Request, Snoop, Response and Data channels, and the Home Node as the coherence authority running MOESI semantics [From Specification to Closure: A Semi-Automated Coverage-Driven Verification Methodology for Cache Coherent Home Nodes, 2026].

What makes a NoC hard to verify is that the *interesting* behavior is a function of configuration, not just RTL. Modern NoCs rely on configurable **System Address Maps (SAMs)** to express connectivity, routing paths and access permissions across multi-core processors, memory subsystems and accelerators — and each SAM configuration is a distinct operational scenario, with fragmented address spaces, reserved regions and hashing multiplying the verification space combinatorially [Scalable Formal Verification Framework for NoC System Address Map Configurations, 2026]. Layer on multi-die packaging — Intel's Agilex disaggregates IPs across dies and defines a scalable NoC topology to glue them together [Data-Driven Design for Adaptive Multi-Die SoC, 2024] — and the fabric stops being a single chip's problem entirely.

## How it's used in practice

The field splits cleanly into simulation-first and formal-first camps, and the serious teams run both. On the simulation side, the entry point is a **Transaction Level Model in SystemC** used for architecture exploration: you configure topology, arbitration and flow control, drop in nodes, then read latency, throughput, power and thermal analysis views to find the bottleneck link before a single line of RTL exists [System design exploration with fully customizable NoC, 2025]. Co-simulation extends this to real software: Papagrigoriou and Piperaki ran a NoC firewall DUT as a behavioral SystemC model alongside a full FPGA prototype of the same device, bridged by a hybrid TCP/shared-memory asynchronous channel synchronized to a real-time clock [A Hybrid Channel for Co-Simulation of Behavioral SystemC IP with its Full System Prototype on FPGA, 2018]. At the protocol layer, layered UVM agents modeled on the OSI stack handle the new MIPI, PCIe and USB generations that all converge on layered architectures [Verifying Layered Protocols - Leveraging Advanced UVM Capabilities, 2013], and Intel's modem teams used Portable Stimulus to drive complex multithreaded low-power scenarios across a heterogeneous NoC on emulation [Low Power Validation on Emulation Using Portable Stimulus Standard, 2019]. When waveforms and logs balloon, big-data and AI techniques are now applied to mine the standard verification outputs for root cause [Efficient application of AI algorithms for large-scale verification environments based on NoC architecture, 2024].

The formal camp attacks the problems simulation cannot reach exhaustively. The hardest classes — **deadlock, livelock and starvation** — are exactly where simulation struggles, and formal verification of full multi-router networks hits state-space explosion. Nvidia's response is a **hierarchical methodology** built on divide-and-conquer with abstraction at multiple levels, sharing abstract models, progress checking and end-to-end model checking to sign off the whole NoC rather than a few localized properties [Hierarchical Formal Verification and Progress Checking of Network-On-Chip Design, 2025]. Ahmed et al. push the same direction with **path decomposition** plus compositional reasoning and packet abstraction to prove routing micro-architecture correctness at scale [Scaling Formal Verification of Network On Chip Using Path Decomposition, 2026]. For the specific and notorious deadlock case, Chatterjee et al. generate a **FIFO VC dependency graph** from the RTL and use it to verify that no cyclic dependency can form across the VC network [Mechanism to Generate FIFO VC Dependency Graph and Its Application to System Level Deadlock Verification, 2021].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The recurring pitfall is scale. "Formal verification, though capable of exhaustive proofs, faces convergence barriers due to state-space explosion in large multi-router networks," which is why path decomposition and hierarchical abstraction are not optional refinements but the only way the proof finishes [Scaling Formal Verification of Network On Chip Using Path Decomposition, 2026]. System-level **deadlock** bugs are the most expensive symptom: they are "extremely elusive and can often escape pre-silicon verification and even emulation testing," and in the absence of a software or hardware workaround "a possible re-spin could cost the chip manufacturer millions of dollars" [Mechanism to Generate FIFO VC Dependency Graph, 2021]. Configuration explosion is the quieter cousin: each SAM is its own scenario, so fragmented address spaces, reserved regions and hashing produce a verification space that no directed plan can enumerate, which is why Soosai et al. frame SAM verification as a formal problem from the start [Scalable Formal Verification Framework for NoC System Address Map Configurations, 2026]. Cache-coherent NoCs add another axis — out-of-order multi-channel Home Node flows under AMBA CHI create a vast per-opcode permutation space that demands semi-automated coverage-driven closure [From Specification to Closure, 2026]. A practical side-effect of NoC size is that the RTL of hard macros is often unavailable when integration verification must start, and plugging a UVM-based HM model in their place is how teams keep the SoC schedule moving [SoC Verification Enablement Using HM Model, 2019].

Where is this heading? Hierarchical formal plus path decomposition is consolidating as the signoff path for routing and liveness; AI- and big-data-assisted debug is becoming mainstream as waveform databases outgrow human triage; and the multi-die chiplet wave — Intel's disaggregated Agilex, UCIe-connected 3D stacks — is pushing the NoC across die boundaries and into the same verification territory as chiplet interconnect. The NoC stopped being a single-IP problem years ago; it is now the spine of the whole system, and the verification flows are catching up.
