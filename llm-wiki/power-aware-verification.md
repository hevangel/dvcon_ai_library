# Power-Aware Verification

> *Your RTL works. Your UPF is clean. So you fire up the simulator, hit run, and... everything reads as X. The power domain was off and your testbench was happily driving it. Power-aware verification is the missing middle between "the power intent is well-formed" and "the chip behaves correctly when that intent is actually exercised" — it is the practice of making your testbench, your stimulus, and your checks *aware* that power is a first-class citizen. Modern SoCs have hundreds of collapsible power domains and dozens of mutually-exclusive power modes, and your simulation must understand that a register read means something different at 0.7 V than at 1.0 V, that a power state is not just a label but an executable condition. So how do you make a UVM environment speak power fluently, without drowning in static power state tables and brittle hand-coded sequences? Let's dig in.*

## What it is

**Power-aware verification** is the practice of running functional verification *with the power-management infrastructure active* — supply nets are modeled, isolation cells actually clamp, retention registers actually save and restore, and corruption semantics are honored when a domain powers down. It is distinct from low-power *static* verification: the focus here is dynamic behavior across modes, transitions, and the host of side effects that power management introduces into functional stimulus [Power Aware Models: Overcoming barriers in Power Aware Simulation, 2014].

The conceptual backbone is the **power state**. UPF's `add_power_state` command lets designers attach named, refinable operating modes to power domains, supply sets, and ports — and these states drive everything downstream: isolation and level-shifting analysis, power estimation, and the legal transition graph a verification environment must cover [Unleashing the Full Power of UPF Power States, 2015]. Power states compose hierarchically: a top-level domain's state depends on its child domains' states, which in turn depend on supply-set and control-signal states [Let's DisCOVER Power States, 2015]. Coverage of those combinations — not just per-domain states but their *crosses* — is what tells you whether your regression has actually exercised the modes that matter [Cross Coverage of Power States, 2016].

The defining tension in this discipline is the move away from the legacy **power state table (PST)**. PSTs enumerated legal combinations of port states as a flat table — fine for a few domains, catastrophic at SoC scale. IEEE 1801 now marks PST as legacy and replaces it with incrementally refinable `add_power_state` semantics, which classify power states as definite, deferred (available only after implementation UPF is present), or indefinite [Free Yourself from the Tyranny of Power State Tables with Incrementally Refinable UPF, 2017]. The new model preserves the ability to detect illegal states and illegal transitions, which static PST analysis could not do reliably.

## How it's used in practice

A power-aware testbench has to solve three problems: model the analog reality of power, drive stimulus that respects it, and check that the design's behavior is correct under it. The first problem is acute for hard IPs and regulators, which often ship without usable power-aware simulation models. Teams build **power-aware models** by capturing the relevant electrical property — usually voltage or current — as a behavioral value in Liberty or Verilog, exposed through attributes like `pg_pin` and `power_down_function` [Power Aware Models: Overcoming barriers in Power Aware Simulation, 2014]. Where IP vendors ship only a Liberty shell, the behavioral HDL carries no power information, and the verification team must supply it externally.

Stimulus and checking then layer on top. **Power-state coverage** collects hits on individual power states and their transitions, while **cross coverage** captures combinations — a top-level domain's state crossed with its subsystems' states, for example [Cross Coverage of Power States, 2016] [Low Power Coverage: The Missing Piece in Dynamic Simulation, 2018]. Because power states are not ordinary finite-state machines, building a coverage model requires semantics that the standard tooling still lacks; teams assemble their own bin definitions and transition trackers from UPF and HDL objects [Low Power Coverage: The Missing Piece in Dynamic Simulation, 2018]. To avoid drowning in tables, a discovery-based methodology uses UPF objects directly — covering supply-set states, control-signal states, and strategy conditions rather than hand-coding a flat state space [Let's DisCOVER Power States, 2015].

The newer flows reach for **formal techniques** to complement simulation. Synopsys's VCLP-to-VC-Formal cross-technology flow uses a power-aware formal property together with a power network model (PNM) to prove connectivity and functional properties that would be infeasible to cover dynamically [Paradigm Shift in Power Aware Simulation Using Formal Techniques, 2023]. Qualcomm reports shifting critical low-power bug-catching left by validating UPF instrumentation in formal before running the long subsystem and SoC simulations that previously took days [Left shift catching of critical low power bugs with Formal Verification, 2022]. Power-aware CDC verification is its own sub-discipline, accounting for the impact of power components on clock-domain-crossing analysis [Power Aware CDC Verification at RTL for Faster SoC Verification Closure, 2014] [Advanced Techniques to Accomplish Power Aware CDC Verification, 2018].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The classic pitfall is **X-optimism**. Power-aware simulation injects additional Xs from powered-down regions, and traditional verification techniques — tool-generated assertions, custom bind checkers — cannot catch the resulting hidden bugs. Plain X-propagation is no better: it lacks the power intent, generates noise, and is "not very useful" in a power-aware flow [Is your Power Aware design really x-aware ?, 2014]. The remedy is power-aware X-propagation that knows which Xs are real corruption and which are noise.

Coverage is the other recurring pain. Power designs use ifdef macros, mix power-aware and non-power-aware macro models, and have to run in both low-power and functional modes — so plan-to-closure requires covering power objects *and* achieving meaningful code coverage across the ifdef variants [Unveil the Mystery of Code Coverage in Low-Power Designs: Achieving Power Aware Verification Closure, 2018]. As designs grow past 100 collapsible domains, simulation-only closure becomes infeasible, which is why the field is converging on hybrid simulation-plus-formal flows and shift-left instrumentation.

The direction of travel is open and Python-flavored. The CPAS framework brings power-aware simulation to Cocotb and Yosys, breaking the dependence on commercial simulators for power intent [CPAS: Cocotb Power Aware Simulation Framework, 2024]. Dependency-injection patterns are being applied to power-aware tests to manage the combinatorics of which sequences run in which modes [Using Dependency Injection Design Pattern in Power Aware Tests, 2021]. And the slow displacement of PSTs by `add_power_state` continues, with tools automating the conversion so legacy flows can still consume the new semantics [Power State to PST Conversion: Simplifying static analysis and debugging of power aware designs, 2016].
