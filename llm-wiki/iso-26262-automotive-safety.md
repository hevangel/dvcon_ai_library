# ISO 26262 — Automotive Functional Safety

> *"Your car's brakes cannot phone home for a patch. When a bit flips in the microcontroller running adaptive cruise control, the chip has to detect it, flag it, and drive itself into a safe state — all before the driver ever notices, and all before the 'fault tolerant time interval' runs out. That is the bet ISO 26262 forces on every automotive semiconductor: prove, with quantified metrics, that no single-point hardware failure can harm the driver. The numbers are stark — ASIL D demands a Single-Point Fault Metric above 90% and a Latent Fault Metric above 80%, measured across a fault campaign that can run into millions of injected faults. NXP, Infineon, Hyundai, Samsung, and AMD have all stood up at DVCon to share how they actually close those numbers, and the story is rarely 'we simulated it.' It is a hybrid of safety synthesis, formal fault analysis, emulation, and increasingly a virtualized CPU bolted onto an RTL emulator. So what does an ISO 26262 safety flow actually look like in 2026, and why is it still so hard? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**ISO 26262** is the international standard for the functional safety of automotive electrical and electronic systems; it defines functional safety as "the absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of E/E systems" [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019]. The standard opens with a **Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA)** that scores each hazard on severity, exposure, and controllability, and assigns one of four **Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASIL A through D)** — ASIL D being the most stringent, where a single failure could cause catastrophic, uncontrollable harm [What-If analysis of Safety Mechanism's impacts on ETHMAC design under Functional Safety flow, 2022]. Each safety goal is then implemented through **safety mechanisms** — hardware (parity, ECC, lockstep, TMR) or software (test patterns, CRC checks in drivers) — and the whole design is quantified through a **FMEDA** (Failure Modes, Effects, and Diagnostic Analysis).

The quantitative core rests on a **base failure rate** (random failures per billion operating hours, estimated from transistor count per IEC 62380) and three architectural metrics: the **Single-Point Fault Metric (SPFM)**, the **Latent Fault Metric (LFM)**, and the **Probabilistic Metric for Random Hardware Failures (PMHF)** [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025]. ISO 26262 Part 5 strongly recommends fault injection testing for ASIL C and D, and for ASIL B/C/D the SPFM thresholds climb (≥90% at ASIL D) [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019]. Proving those numbers means a **safety flow** that runs in parallel to normal SoC design: safety analysis (define safety mechanisms and estimate diagnostic coverage), safety insertion (implement them in RTL and in device drivers), and safety verification (fault injection tuned to the mechanism type) [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025].

## How it's used in practice

The workhorse is the **fault campaign**. Conventional flows are simulation-based and suffer from exhaustivity problems: with `N` fault locations and `T` clock cycles, the candidate fault space is enormous, so teams sample statistically and then triage what the safety mechanism missed. Ahuja et al. propose using formal to prove that "not observed" faults are untestable by design, closing the diagnostic-coverage gap without brute force [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019]. Ahn et al. push triage further on a Samsung automotive SoC, combining static structural analysis with formal verification to detect and resolve faults early and ensure ASIL diagnostic coverage holds across the software safety mechanisms [Static Structural Analysis and Formal Verification of SoC with Software Safety Mechanisms for Functional Safety, 2025].

Formal has become a first-class citizen of the safety flow. Yeung, Smith and colleagues describe using static analysis to prune the fault list before injection, then formal fault injection to maximize observability of safety failures [Whose Fault Is It Formally?, 2018], and a follow-up safety-synthesis flow that inserts safety mechanisms, validates them with formal equivalence, and measures diagnostic coverage with formal fault injection [Are You Safe Yet?, 2020]. Smith's "lessons learned" paper formalizes two roles: verifying safety functionality (or proving RTL equivalence when mechanisms are inserted) and generating fault lists for random campaigns, alone or in tandem with simulation and emulation [Lessons Learned Using Formal for Functional Safety, 2025]. For integration, Busch describes Infineon's library of formally pre-verified safety components and the rules for inserting them across AURIX microcontroller modules [Automatic Insertion of a Safety Mechanism for Registers in RTL-Modules, 2024] [Integration Verification of Safety Components in Automotive Chip Modules, 2023].

The newest wrinkle is **software safety mechanisms**, which only execute when the whole SoC runs together — typically near project end. Nabil, Saied and Makram's hybrid approach bolts a virtualized CPU host onto an RTL emulator of the peripheral IP through a transaction-level interface, getting 10× speedup over full emulation and 1000× over RTL sim, with only 0.12% resource use; their SPI controller hit 96.3% fault detection, matching ISO 26262's own estimates [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025]. Hyundai and Synopsys have pushed FMEDA itself up to RTL so single-point and latent faults surface before gate-level simulation, correlating RTL metrics back to GLS for accurate architectural analysis [Early FMEDA at RTL for Functional Safety, 2026].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The recurring pain is **campaign runtime and coverage justification**. A large automotive SoC generates millions of candidate faults, and waiting for gate-level data to size the campaign blows the schedule; Yim, Garg and Um show how conducting FMEDA at RTL — assessing safety metrics, running fault injections, and analyzing diagnostic coverage much earlier — lets designers address single-point and latent faults before implementation and correlate RTL metrics back to GLS for accurate architectural analysis [Early FMEDA at RTL for Functional Safety, 2026]. Coverage justification — proving that safety mechanisms *comprehensively* protect every safety-relevant element — is the harder problem: Baudoin and Garg argue that **elementary subpart (ESP) granularity** is the foundation of meaningful safety analysis, and that identifying architectural weaknesses early, before RTL campaigns, is where suppliers actually save their schedules [Breaking Down Failures, 2026].

The field is converging on **early, hybrid, metric-driven safety**. The trajectory runs from gate-level-only FMEDA toward RTL FMEDA correlated to GLS [Early FMEDA at RTL for Functional Safety, 2026]; from pure simulation fault injection toward formal-assisted and emulation-accelerated campaigns [Lessons Learned Using Formal for Functional Safety, 2025]; and from hardware-only mechanisms toward software mechanisms validated on hybrid emulation+virtualization platforms [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025]. What-If analyses of how different mechanisms trade off area, diagnostic coverage, and latency on a real block like ETHMAC are now routine inputs to architecture decisions [What-If analysis of Safety Mechanism's impacts on ETHMAC design, 2022]. ISO 26262 has stopped being a paperwork exercise and become a quantitative engineering discipline — and the chip that ships is the one whose numbers actually close.
