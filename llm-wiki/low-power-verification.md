# Low-Power Verification with UPF

> *You wrote the RTL. It simulates. It even passes coverage. Then someone powers down half the chip, and the isolation cell you forgot to wire clamps a critical reset line to ground, your retention register never gets its save signal, and three weeks of bring-up evaporate in a puff of Xs. Welcome to low-power verification — the part of the chip where "it works" suddenly depends on whether a power switch flipped in the right order, on whether the right Liberty `pg_pin` attribute was filled in, on whether your UPF and your RTL actually agree about which domain owns which flop. A modern SoC can have thirty power domains and over four thousand legal power states, and you have to convince yourself the silicon survives every one. So how did we end up with a verification discipline that has its own IEEE standard, its own coverage model, and its own class of bugs that only appear when the lights go out? Let's dig in.*

## What it is

**Low-power verification** is the discipline of confirming that a chip still functions correctly when its power-management infrastructure is exercised — when domains switch on and off, when **isolation cells** clamp signals at boundaries, when **retention registers** snapshot and restore state, and when **level shifters** bridge voltage islands. The technology is driven by IEEE 1801, the **Unified Power Format (UPF)**, which lets designers capture this power intent in a side file separate from the RTL [Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice, 2010]. Without UPF, the same RTL would have to be forked for every power configuration; with it, one design can be exercised against many intent files.

The scope is wider than newcomers expect. Traditional low-power tricks — clock gating, multi-threshold logic, cell optimization — leave the power network untouched. The hard problems begin with advanced techniques like multi-VDD, power gating, voltage scaling, and VDD-standby, all of which restructure the supply network itself [Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice, 2010]. Power states transcend hierarchy: a single chip-level mode is a combination of states drawn from dozens of domains, supplies, and control sequences, and the legal combinations have to be enumerated, verified, and covered.

What makes low-power verification a discipline of its own is that the design now has *two* descriptions — the functional RTL and the power-management UPF — and both must be exercised together in **power-aware simulation**, not pure RTL simulation [UPF Code Coverage and Corresponding Power Domain Hierarchical Tree for Debugging, 2015]. The flow also depends on library infrastructure: Liberty attributes like `pg_pin`, `related_power_pin`, and `power_down_function` carry the power semantics that simulators need, and in practice library providers ship only partial sets, which forces verification teams to audit libraries before they can trust a single result [Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice, 2010].

## How it's used in practice

A mature low-power flow runs on three legs: **static analysis**, **dynamic (power-aware) simulation**, and **coverage closure**. Static tools check the UPF for consistency, isolation/retention correctness, and corruption semantics beyond what a linter can see — power states cross hierarchies, so per-block functional checks are not enough [Low Power Static Verification- Beyond Linting and Corruption Semantics, 2011]. Dynamic simulation then drives the design through real power sequences, with assertions bound to UPF objects via query functions and `bind_checker` constructs to validate control logic before, during, and after mode transitions [Low-Power Verification Methodology using UPF Query functions and Bind checkers, 2014].

The combinatorics are brutal. A single chip can mix dozens of power domains, retention memories, clock sources, reset domains, wakeup sources, and abort scenarios, and the total verification space is the product of all of them [Complex Low Power Verification Challenges in NextGen SoCs: Taming the Beast!, 2014]. Closing that space requires a directed-random coverage methodology — the UPF 3.0 **information model** exposes power objects so that coverage can be collected on domain transitions, supply-set states, and strategy conditions, not just on functional state machines [Random Directed Low Power Coverage Methodology: A Smart Approach to Power Aware Verification Closure, 2017]. Tools like UPVM (Unified Power Verification Methodology) bridge UVM and UPF, and Python-based automation is increasingly used to generate the boilerplate UPF and UPVM glue that would otherwise be hand-written [Optimizing UPF Integration Efficiency through Enabling Automation with UPVM for Unified Power Verification, 2024] [Auto Generation is the key to rapid integration to UPF-like UPVM libraries for Unified Power Verification, 2024].

The flow must also keep moving as the design matures. Gate-level simulation verifies the implemented netlist against UPF and depends on cell-library power attributes that may not match the RTL assumptions; supply-network connectivity gaps surface only at GLS, where incomplete `related_power_pin` or `power_down_function` data quietly produces wrong corruption behavior [Supply network connectivity: An imperative part in low power gate-level verification, 2019]. Top-level primary IOs are especially treacherous — they are usually driven by off-chip supplies whose attribution is ambiguous, and getting it wrong either corrupts signals that should be clean or fails to corrupt signals that should be clamped [Primary, Anonymous, or What?, 2021].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The standing pitfalls are well documented. Incomplete or wrong Liberty attributes waste days of debug before anyone realizes the bug is in the library, not the design [Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice, 2010]. Power-state coverage for a thirty-domain design with 4,000+ legal states is itself an open problem, and planning intent for high-level power transitions is still largely manual [Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice, 2010]. **X-optimism** — where simulation hides real bugs by propagating unknowns optimistically — gets worse in power-aware flows because powered-down regions inject additional Xs, and traditional bind checkers cannot catch the resulting hidden failures [Is your Power Aware design really x-aware ?, 2014].

The trajectory of the field is toward **shift-left** validation and tooling that treats UPF as a programmable artifact rather than a hand-maintained file. Virtual-instrumentation-based predictive analysis now validates physical-aware generated UPF before it reaches simulation [Next-Gen Low Power Verification: Empowering Shift-Left Predictive Analysis with Virtual Instrumentation, 2024]. **Low Power Apps** packaged on top of UPF 3.0 expose reusable, queryable verification capability so teams can assemble flows from composable units instead of rebuilding them per project [Low Power Apps: Shaping the Future of Low Power Verification, 2018] [A New Approach to Low-Power Verification: Low Power Apps, 2018]. And the slow migration of static power-state tables (PSTs) toward the more expressive `add_power_state` semantics finally gives teams a way to refine and reuse power-state definitions across abstraction levels [Power State to PST Conversion: Simplifying static analysis and debugging of power aware designs, 2016].

## Grounded in these DVCon papers

- **Low Power Verification with UPF: Principle and Practice** (2010, DVCon US) — Jianfeng Liu, Mi-Sook Hong, Bong Hyun Lee and JungYun Choi. The foundational articulation of low-power verification principles, library attributes, and the open problems of a 30-power-domain design.
- **Random Directed Low Power Coverage Methodology: A Smart Approach to Power Aware Verification Closure** (2017, DVCon US) — Awashesh Kumar and Madhur Bhargava. A directed-random coverage methodology built on the UPF 3.0 low-power information model.
- **Probing UPF Dynamic Objects: Methodologies to Build Your Custom Low-Power Verification Platform** (2020, DVCon Europe) — Progyna Khondkar. Techniques for continuously monitoring the dynamic state of power domains, supply sets, and strategies.
- **UPF Code Coverage and Corresponding Power Domain Hierarchical Tree for Debugging** (2015, DVCon US) — Shang-Wei Tu, Tom Lin and Archie Feng. Argues that low-power verification is incomplete without UPF-aware code coverage alongside RTL coverage.
- **Next-Gen Low Power Verification: Empowering Shift-Left Predictive Analysis with Virtual Instrumentation** (2024, DVCon India) — Sachin Bansal, Yi Liu and M. Vaishnavi Reddy. Shifts UPF validation left using physical-aware predictive analysis.
- **Optimizing UPF Integration Efficiency through Enabling Automation with UPVM for Unified Power Verification** (2024, DVCon Japan) — Gopi Srinivas Deepala, Lakshya Miglaini and Sastry Puranapanda. Python automation that bridges UPF power strategies with UPVM class libraries.
- **Low-Power Verification Methodology using UPF Query functions and Bind checkers** (2014, DVCon Europe) — Madhur Bhargava and Durgesh Prasad. Uses UPF query functions and bind checkers to validate power-control sequences with assertions.
- **Complex Low Power Verification Challenges in NextGen SoCs: Taming the Beast!** (2014, DVCon Europe) — Abhinav Nawal and Gaurav Jain. Catalogs the combinatorial explosion of domains, clocks, resets, and wakeup sources that defines NextGen SoC verification.
- **Low Power Static Verification- Beyond Linting and Corruption Semantics** (2011, DVCon US) — Kaustav Guha, Ankush Bagotra and Neha Bajaj. Extends static low-power analysis beyond linting to hierarchical, chip-level power-state semantics.
- **Auto Generation is the key to rapid integration to UPF-like UPVM libraries for Unified Power Verification** (2024, DVCon Europe) — Gopi Srinivas Deepala, Lakshya Miglani and Himanshu Vishwakarma. Python-based generation of UPF/UPVM constructs across the RTL-to-GDSII flow.
- **Primary, Anonymous, or What? The Destiny of Ports from Design Top that Ultimately are Driven from Off-Chip** (2021, DVCon US) — Brandon Skaggs and Progyna Khondkar. Tackles the ambiguous attribution of top-level primary IO supplies.
- **Supply network connectivity: An imperative part in low power gate-level verification** (2019, DVCon US) — Vinay Kumar Singh and Gabriel Chidolue. Why GLS depends on cell-library power attributes and supply-network completeness.
- **Low Power Apps: Shaping the Future of Low Power Verification** (2018, DVCon US) — Awashesh Kumar, Madhur Bhargava and Vinay Singh. Composable, queryable apps built on the UPF 3.0 information model.
- **A New Approach to Low-Power Verification: Low Power Apps** (2018, DVCon Europe) — Madhur Bhargava and Awashesh Kumar. Extends the Low Power Apps vision for early, catch-the-bug coverage.
- **Power State to PST Conversion: Simplifying static analysis and debugging of power aware designs** (2016, DVCon US) — Madhur Bhargava and Pankaj Gairola. The migration path from legacy PSTs to `add_power_state` semantics.
