# Ethernet and Networking MAC Verification

> *"Ethernet is older than most of the engineers verifying it, and it refuses to sit still. What began as a 10 Mb/s shared-medium LAN is now a stack spanning 10 Mb/s automotive OAM all the way to 1.6 Tb/s data-center links, with PCS, FEC, and MAC layers that change shape at every speed grade. The 802.3 spec is thousands of pages of timing diagrams and codeword markers, and that is *before* you bolt Audio Video Bridging and Time-Sensitive Networking on top for in-vehicle use, or the Ultra Ethernet Consortium's new layers for AI-scale fabrics. Sameh El-Ashry at Cadence just published a paper on building a *single* configurable UVM environment that covers a generic Ethernet controller from 10 Mb to 1.6T, because reworking the testbench by hand for every RTL variant has become economically impossible [A Novel Configurable UVM Architecture To Unlock 1.6T Ethernet Verification, 2025]. Automotive teams have their own pain: Gandhi and colleagues debugged packet losses between a Linux VLAN app and an AUTOSAR ECU across a virtualized Ethernet switch and found no single tool gave them a holistic view of the multi-hop traffic [Accelerating Automotive Ethernet validation, 2020]. And underneath every Ethernet link is a SerDes and a CDR that have to lock at multi-gigabit rates. So what's actually going on under the hood?"*

## What it is — the Ethernet stack at every speed

An **Ethernet controller** is layered: a **MAC** (Media Access Control) handles framing, addressing, and error detection; a **PCS (Physical Coding Sublayer)** does 64b/66b encoding and lane alignment; a **PMA/PHY** drives the physical medium. As speeds climb, Forward Error Correction (FEC) variants, the number of SerDes lanes, and the codeword-marker alignment rules all multiply the configuration space, so that "verification signoff of Ethernet Design IP with vast configuration related to Speed, type of FEC encoding, number of Serdes lanes has considerable challenges" [Comprehensive & Configurable Ethernet IP Verification Strategy, 2025]. At the top of the stack sit higher-level protocols — **Audio Video Bridging (AVB)** and **Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN)** for automotive, **Software Defined Networking (SDN)** for switches, and the new **Ultra Ethernet Consortium (UEC)** layers for AI fabrics — each of which has its own stateful behavior on top of the MAC.

Two structural facts shape verification. First, the spec's simplified state machine "fails to reflect the parallel operation of memory banks" and similar real-world concurrency, so engineers end up building more expressive models of their own [DRAMPyML, 2025 — modeling philosophy transferred]. Second, the analog layer cannot be ignored: an IEEE 802.3bw automotive PHY "requires analog/mixed signal circuits, pulling verification into the mixed signal domain" and a common UVM test environment is insufficient on its own [Protocol verification of an IEEE 802.3bw PHY, 2018]. Ethernet, in other words, is a stack where every layer breaks differently.

## How it's used in practice

The recurring practical pattern is **a configurable, multi-platform verification strategy** rather than a single environment. Stojanovic and Misic's *perfect blend* paper lays out the flow used on an Ethernet Subsystem, where each segment of the verification plan is matched to the platform that suits it — formal verification for property-style checks, RTL and gate-level simulation for functional coverage, emulation for performance, and FPGA for hardware-in-the-loop — with strong emphasis on reuse of testbench components across them [A perfect blend of verification techniques, platforms, and AI-empowered debugging in Ethernet Subsystem Verification, 2023]. El-Ashry's configurable UVM environment automates the testbench-customization problem for a 10 Mb-to-1.6T controller: a Python-based builder flow consumes RTL defines (optional UEC layers, variable SerDes widths, data rates) and emits a matching testbench configuration, avoiding manual rework for each RTL variant [A Novel Configurable UVM Architecture To Unlock 1.6T Ethernet Verification, 2025]. O'Connor, El-Ashry and Ansari combine UVM with Python-based modeling and formal verification (Cadence Jasper) to sign off blocks traditionally considered not formal-friendly — for example, **counter abstraction on RX aligners with FSM state correlation** for codeword-marker detection [Comprehensive & Configurable Ethernet IP Verification Strategy, 2025].

**Portable Stimulus (PSS)** is the other workhorse for high-capacity Ethernet bridges and endpoints. Vintila and Tolea layer PSS on top of SystemVerilog/UVM to verify a highly configurable, high-speed communication bridge, using PSS to avoid "scenario flooding" and keep tight control of the verification space while still constructing directed/random scenarios from a generation model [Portable Stimulus Driven SystemVerilog/UVM verification environment for the verification of a high-capacity Ethernet communication bridge, 2019]. For automotive Ethernet, **virtual prototyping** is the dominant tool: Gandhi et al. build a SystemC TLM VDK with multiple virtual ECUs connected through an Ethernet switch and integrate it with the TraceCompass Eclipse plugin for correlated multi-node analysis — because no single pcap capture can correlate traffic across the many in-vehicle hops [Accelerating Automotive Ethernet validation, 2020].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The dominant pitfall is **multi-node, cross-layer visibility**. Debugging an Ethernet path in simulation is hard because "there is no tool with a holistic view of Ethernet transactions traversal across multiple in-vehicle hops" [Accelerating Automotive Ethernet validation, 2020]. Wireshark and pcap captures help on a single node but cannot be correlated across a system. The 802.3bw work makes the same point at the PHY level: combining digital and analog circuits in a single UVM environment is a non-trivial problem whose solution matters for time-to-market and verification quality [Protocol verification of an IEEE 802.3bw PHY, 2018]. At switch scale, configurability itself becomes the trap: multi-terabit SDN devices have configuration spaces that exceed the practical limits of traditional verification, which is why Shrivastava et al. turned to formal methodology on Cavium's XPliant Ethernet switch [Formal Verification of Silicon for Software Defined Networking, 2018].

The field is heading toward **AI-assisted debug and TLM-based virtualization**. Stojanovic and Misic report that an AI-empowered debugging platform surfaces anomalies in instruction-trace address values over time, bringing new ideas for bandwidth and traffic-balancing improvements [A perfect blend, 2023]. Virtual ECUs built with QEMU and SystemC TLM-2.0 are scaling automotive verification beyond what hardware testbenches can support [Virtual ECUs with QEMU and SystemC TLM-2.0, 2023]. And there is an open modeling question: the original TLM-2.0 standard was designed for memory-mapped buses, and "using TLM-2.0 for other model-to-model interfaces such as interrupt signals, serial protocols, and Ethernet-like protocols... doesn't work very well" — the TLM Working Group is extending the standard to fix this [TLM Beyond Memory Mapped Busses, 2016]. Layered-protocol stimulus generation has its own history here, with early OVM work showing how to handle multi-layer protocols like IP-over-Ethern by generating stimulus at whichever layer the error needs injecting [Effects of Abstraction in Stimulus Generation of Layered Protocols within OVM, 2010]. As Ethernet stretches from in-car 100BASE-T1 to 1.6T AI fabrics, the verification stack is being rebuilt at both ends simultaneously.
