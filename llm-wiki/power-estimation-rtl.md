# RTL Power Estimation and Analysis

> *"Silicon has a wall-plug number, and the team that doesn't hit it loses the socket. But you don't get to measure that number until the chip comes back from the fab — months late, millions of dollars in — so you'd better estimate it well beforehand. That's the whole game of RTL power estimation: feed a power tool a synthesized netlist, a switching-activity file, and a Liberty library, and get back a number you can defend in a design review. The catch is that switching activity is everything. The same RTL can dissipate 3× more power under one workload than another, and the only way to capture that is to run realistic scenarios and dump what toggled. Intel's Rolla and Modi were fighting display power estimation at 8K in 2020; Samsung's Park et al. were profiling full-chip power under OS boot on an emulator in 2017; NXP's Genov was pushing power analysis up to ESL with SystemC/TLM2.0 the same decade. The unifying question is always the same: how do you get an accurate power number before silicon exists, without waiting until silicon exists to afford it? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**RTL power estimation** is the discipline of predicting a chip's power dissipation from its RTL (or gate-level netlist) before silicon exists. The fundamental equation is `P(t) = V(t) × I(t)`, with voltage roughly constant, so the whole job comes down to estimating current — which splits into **static (leakage) power** from transistor subthreshold and gate-tunneling currents, and **dynamic power** from the switching activity of transistors as they charge and discharge capacitance [Mixed Electronic System Level Power/Performance Estimation using SystemC/TLM2.0 Modeling and PwClkARCH Library, 2020]. The power tool needs three inputs to produce a number: a synthesized netlist with technology cells, a Liberty library that characterizes each cell's power per transition, and a **switching activity** file that records what toggled, when, and how often.

Switching activity is captured in industry-standard interchange formats — **SAIF** (Switching Activity Interchange Format), **VCD** (Value Change Dump), and **FSDB** (Fast Signal Database) — produced by simulation or emulation runs over the workloads you care about [Netlist Enabled Emulation Platform for Accelerated Gate Level Verification, 2023]. The accuracy of the final number is bounded by how representative that activity is: a single-frame test on a display controller might say one thing, but the actual wall-plug number depends on the resolution and FPS the customer runs, and generating the activity for an 8K frame through RTL simulation can take one to two weeks [Efficient Methods for Display Power Estimation and Visualization, 2020]. The UPF (IEEE 1801) power intent sits alongside this, telling the tool which domains are on, which are gated, and what voltage each is at — without which the static-vs-dynamic split is meaningless [Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification -What have we learned?, 2015].

## How it's used in practice

Three flows dominate, chosen by trading accuracy against runtime. The **RTL simulation flow** runs the workload on the RTL, dumps SAIF/VCD/FSDB, feeds the activity and a synthesized netlist to a power tool like PrimeTime PX, and reads the report — accurate enough for IP-level work, but it scales badly: Intel's display team watched single-frame waveform generation slow from hours at 320p to weeks at 8K [Efficient Methods for Display Power Estimation and Visualization, 2020]. The **emulation flow** replaces RTL simulation with FPGA-based emulation, generating the same FSDB/SAIF but 7–14× faster — Samsung's Park et al. run realistic OS-boot and Android-application scenarios on a full-chip emulator, then use a "power critical signal" flow to dump only the FSDB for regions of interest, compressing profiled files up to 241× and speeding post-processing up to 176× [EMULATION BASED FULL CHIP LEVEL LOW POWER VALIDATION AT PRE-SILICON STAGE, 2017]. The **ESL flow** pushes estimation even earlier, modeling power and performance together in SystemC/TLM2.0 with libraries like PwClkARCH, applied by Genov et al. to an NXP switch matrix used in i.MX8 SoCs [Mixed Electronic System Level Power/Performance Estimation using SystemC/TLM2.0 Modeling and PwClkARCH Library, 2020].

A common refinement is **split-run SAIF** to bound runtime on long workloads. Rolla and Modi automate the splitting so a desired activity window — say, 12–16 ms into the run — gets dumped without paying for the full waveform before it, achieving 4× speedup on 4-split runs and 8× on 8-split runs while still producing peak and average power plots plus per-partition switching-activity views for finding hotspots [Efficient Methods for Display Power Estimation and Visualization, 2020]. At the SoC level, power-aware modeling has to be reusable: Genov et al. describe a system-level approach that follows UPF semantics but lets the power-intent description start earlier than RTL, so the same model can be refined down through RTL and gate level [Reusable System-level Power-aware IP Modeling Approach, 2022]. And for gate-level accuracy, Pottem et al. at Samsung deploy a Netlist Enabled Emulation Platform for accelerated gate-level verification — including SAIF-based power estimation — because RTL can't catch the delays and switching-activity nuances that gate-level nets expose [Netlist Enabled Emulation Platform for Accelerated Gate Level Verification, 2023].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The first pitfall is **mismatched abstraction between power and logic**. Parikh's comparative study, run across multiple EDA vendors and multiple abstraction levels (behavioral, RTL, gate), shows that the same design produces materially different power numbers depending on which flow you pick — and that there is no single "right" answer, only tradeoffs the team has to understand [Power Estimation Techniqueswhat to expect, what not to expect, 2014]. The second pitfall is **representative activity**: a power number is only as good as the workload that produced it, and the workloads that matter (OS boot, real applications) are too long to run at RTL speed — which is what keeps pushing teams to emulation and ESL. Nawal and Jain capture the underlying complexity: next-gen SoCs stack fine-grained clock gating, multi-domain power shutoff, body biasing, and DVFS, all of which change the switching activity and the leakage picture from one mode to the next, so the power estimate has to be re-run across all of them [Complex Low Power Verification Challenges in NextGen SoCs: Taming the Beast!, 2014].

The field is heading toward **ESL-early, emulation-fast, UPF-aware estimation**. UPF 3.0 (released 2015) was the inflection point: before it, power estimation required an RTL model, but UPF 3.0 let the power model start at ESL with just power/logical states, then be refined to voltage and supply nets — Genov et al. argue this is what finally makes detailed power/performance design exploration tractable at the abstraction where architects actually make decisions [Mixed Electronic System Level Power/Performance Estimation using SystemC/TLM2.0 Modeling and PwClkARCH Library, 2020]. Coverage is being pulled in too: Bhargava and Gairola show how to convert power states into PSTs to simplify static analysis and debugging, taming the combinatorial explosion of supply-port states and power-mode combinations that modern low-power designs create [Power State to PST Conversion: Simplifying static analysis and debugging of power aware designs, 2016]. The long arc is that power estimation is moving earlier (ESL), getting faster (emulation and split-run SAIF), and getting more rigorous (PST-driven coverage) — because the cost of being wrong at the wall plug is now too high to discover in the lab.
