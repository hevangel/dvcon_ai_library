# CDC (Clock Domain Crossing) Verification

> Take any modern SoC and count the clocks. You will lose interest well before you finish — there are dozens, many of them asynchronous, and every signal that hops between two of them is a place where silicon can quietly die. The phenomenon is metastability: a flip-flop sampled at the wrong moment refuses to settle, and the resulting garbage propagates downstream as a glitch, a corrupted packet, or a chip that intermittently fails in the field. CDC issues are reportedly the second most common reason for silicon re-spins, which is a polite way of saying they cost enormous amounts of money. The discipline that hunts them — structural analysis, protocol checking, metastability modeling, hierarchical signoff — is one of the most mature in verification, and yet every new low-power trick or third-party IP integration finds a fresh way to break it. So how did we get here, and what does "CDC-clean" actually mean?

## What it is and why it matters

A **clock domain crossing (CDC)** occurs whenever a signal generated in one clock domain is sampled in another asynchronous domain, and the crossing can violate the receiving flip-flop's setup or hold time. The result is **metastability** — the inability of a flop to arrive at a known state within a clock period — which cannot be eliminated, only mitigated by inserting an appropriate synchronizer at every CDC path [Systematically Achieving CDC Verification Closure based on Coverage Models and Coverage Metrics, 2012]. The goal of CDC analysis is therefore structural: identify every crossing, classify the synchronizer that protects it, and verify that the synchronizer plus the surrounding logic obey the protocols needed to prevent data loss or corruption [A Systematic Take on Addressing Dynamic CDC Verification Challenges, 2019].

Because CDC bugs are statistical — they manifest only when an unlucky timing alignment occurs — simulation alone cannot catch them. RTL simulation has no model for the probabilistic delay a metastable flop exhibits in real silicon, so the verification stack has to layer in metastability injection models, formal protocol checkers, and structural rule checks. **Verification of Clock Domain Crossing Jitter and Metastability Tolerance using Emulation** spells out the high-level requirements for accurate metastability modeling: it must be injected independently per bit, only when the data actually changes *and* is sampled *and* violates setup or hold, and at every relevant crossing regardless of synchronizer type [Verification of Clock Domain Crossing Jitter and Metastability Tolerance using Emulation, 2012]. Get any of those wrong and your "CDC-verified" badge is meaningless.

## How the methodology has matured

The earliest mature approach was **flat CDC analysis** — flatten the whole SoC and run structural checks end to end. It worked for small chips, but multi-billion-gate SoCs made the volume of CDC data overwhelming and the debug inefficient [Fully Hierarchical CDC Analysis Using Comprehensive CDC Meta Database, 2019]. The fix is hierarchical signoff: each IP is signed off locally, and a **CDC Meta-Database** captures port connectivity, environment constraints, CDC configuration (synchronizer depth, for example), and synchronizer connectivity as a deliverable to the SoC integrator. The database is comprehensive enough to provide flat-equivalent coverage at the top level while still concise enough to deliver performance and capacity gains, and it lets the integrator run consistency checks between the IP-level and SoC-level intent [Fully Hierarchical CDC Analysis Using Comprehensive CDC Meta Database, 2019]. A side benefit is parameterization: one meta-model with default parameters can be automatically regenerated for every instantiation the SoC uses.

The other axis of maturity is coverage and closure. **Systematically Achieving CDC Verification Closure** introduced coverage models and metrics specifically for CDC, turning what used to be an ad hoc waiver grind into a measurable process [Systematically Achieving CDC Verification Closure based on Coverage Models and Coverage Metrics, 2012]. **Implementation of a closed loop CDC verification methodology** extended the traditional flow to automatically filter and verify "false" violations, apply design rule checks to preserve CDC logic from RTL through tapeout, and embed a non-synthesizable behavioral synchronizer in the validation environment so metastability can be checked in simulation without a separate CDC tool license [Implementation of a closed loop CDC verification methodology, 2014]. **Comprehensive IP to SoC Clock Domain Crossing Verification Using Hybrid Data Model** pushed the IP-to-SoC handoff further, recognizing that third-party IP reuse and aggressive schedules demand a flow where SoC teams can integrate pre-verified IP blocks without re-running the internals [Comprehensive IP to SoC Clock Domain Crossing Verification Using Hybrid Data Model, 2018].

Power and safety bring their own wrinkles. **Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification** and its successors show that isolation, retention, and level-shifter cells at power-domain interfaces create new CDC failure modes that conventional analysis misses, and that **Unified Power Format (UPF)** descriptions must feed the CDC flow [Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification, 2015][Advanced Techniques to Accomplish Power Aware CDC Verification, 2018]. **FPGA-based Clock Domain Crossing Validation for Safety-Critical Designs** makes the case that DO-254 and similar safety standards require CDC to be validated on real hardware, not just in simulation, because simulation cannot give the verification completeness criteria those standards demand [FPGA-based Clock Domain Crossing Validation for Safety-Critical Designs, 2019]. **Pragmatic Formal Verification Methodology for CDC** brings formal property checking to the table for Globally-Asynchronous Locally-Synchronous (GALS) designs where RTL simulation and static timing analysis are not enough [Pragmatic Formal Verification Methodology for Clock Domain Crossing (CDC), 2023].

## Glitches, gate-level, and the signoff frontier

Two persistent pain points dominate the recent literature. The first is **glitches** — undesired transitions on combinational or multiplexer logic at a CDC path that get captured before the signal settles. **Preventing Chip-Killing Glitches on CDC Paths with Automated Formal Analysis** calls them the "silent chip-killer" and shows that automated formal glitch detection catches defects that would otherwise surface as a panic weeks before tapeout or, worse, as a costly post-manufacturing recall [Preventing Chip-Killing Glitches on CDC Paths with Automated Formal Analysis, 2018]. The second is gate-level CDC: synthesis can introduce CDC errors that did not exist in RTL, and CDC closure on the gate-level netlist is one of the most difficult tasks in the cycle due to high setup effort, performance cost, and noise. **Accelerating CDC Verification Closure on Gate-Level Designs** reviews the root causes and introduces techniques to make gate-level CDC tractable [Accelerating CDC Verification Closure on Gate-Level Designs, 2017]. **Evolution of CDC recipe** captures the practitioner's view: bottom-up hierarchical abstract-model flows, IP-quality checks, and integration-connection validation against the SoC are where the real bugs hide in production graphics chips [Evolution of CDC recipe, 2021].

Where the field is heading is convergence between CDC, reset-domain, and power-domain verification. **Power Aware CDC Verification at RTL** and the DVFS-aware variant treat power and CDC as a single coupled problem rather than two serial checks [Power Aware CDC Verification at RTL for Faster SoC Verification Closure, 2014][Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015]. The unifying insight is that clock, reset, and power domains are three facets of the same metastability hazard, and verifying them in isolation repeatedly misses the bugs that live at their intersections.
