# Regression Optimization

> *Every night, somewhere on the planet, a verification farm spins up tens of thousands of tests across an LSF cluster, finishes around dawn, and tells you that everything still passes — except you don't quite believe it, because the one corner-case bug you actually care about was sitting in a test you didn't run. Regression is the heartbeat of modern DV, and it is also its biggest resource sink: the moment a design grows past a single block, full regression becomes a scheduling, compute, and triage problem rather than a verification problem. The DVCon corpus over the last fifteen years reads like a slow migration from "buy more farm" to "let an agent decide what to run," and 2026's papers are unambiguous about where this is going. So how did we get from LSF queues to multi-agent regression orchestration? Let's dig in.*

## What regression optimization is, and why it became a discipline

A **regression** is the automated, repeatable execution of a suite of verification tests against successive builds of the design, used to confirm that new changes have not broken previously working behavior and to track verification progress. **Regression optimization** is everything you do to make that regression faster, cheaper, more reliable, or smarter per unit of compute. The discipline emerged because of two compounding pressures: design size keeps growing, and time-to-market does not. Hansson and Granath captured the economic reality bluntly — companies spend ever more on computer farms, "yet there is never enough computer resources," and when management asks why, "the answer is simply 'we run more stuff'" [Optimal Usage of the Computer Farm for Regression Testing, 2016]. The same paper observes the paradox that adding compute rarely produces a measurable schedule improvement, because nobody is measuring resource efficiency.

The structural problem is that classical regression is monolithic and execution-centric. As Choi et al. put it, "conventional monolithic regression systems repeatedly execute massive test sets without considering hierarchical design boundaries or verification scope, resulting in high latency, limited scalability, and inefficient resource utilization" [A 3-tiered agentic AI Framework for Verification Regression, 2026]. Even when regression is automated, the typical pipeline remains linear: failure and coverage signals are siloed, a human manager must interpret and relay them, and that human-in-the-loop creates persistent bottlenecks whenever the design or testbench changes [Multi-Agent Orchestration for Autonomous Regression Management, 2026]. Optimization, in this framing, is not just about running faster — it is about closing the loop so that the regression's own outputs drive its next inputs.

## How teams optimize regression in practice

The toolkit has four layers, and most production flows combine them. The **infrastructure layer** is the regression farm itself: LSF or equivalent load-sharing, multi-core parallelism, and license management. Sharma et al. describe the "holy matrimony" of EDA tool parallelism and compute farm orchestration, where multi-core simulators and LSF scheduling must be co-tuned rather than treated independently [Parallel Computing for Functional Verification and Compute Farms, 2011]. Lacey and Powell frame farm design as a multi-variable optimization over hardware cost, license cost, and use model, and argue for continuous performance tracking rather than one-time sizing [Creating the Optimal Regression Farm Infrastructure, 2018]. Once the farm exists, sim-level optimization kicks in: Kothari et al.'s Centralized Regression Optimization Toolkit (CROT) demonstrates that snapshotting common initialization (PHY, link, controller bring-up across PCIe/CSI/DSI tests) and reusing it across ~2,500 tests cut regression turnaround time by 47-54% [Centralized Regression Optimisation Toolkit, 2021]. Their flow also gates full regression behind a smoke suite that runs first whenever RTL or testbench changes land.

The **selection layer** decides which tests to run given a change. The earliest formalization here is coverage-based clustering: Ikram and Ellis cluster modules by coverage profile, rank associated tests, and dynamically generate per-change regression lists, demonstrating measurable improvement over expert-knowledge-based selection [Dynamic Regression Suite Generation Using Coverage-Based Clustering, 2017]. El Mandouh et al. extend clustering to *good-behavior learning* — grouping passing tests so that a failing test can be flagged as anomalous against its cluster, dramatically reducing debug effort between HW model iterations [Guiding Functional Verification Regression Analysis Using Machine Learning and Big Data Methods, 2018]. Jang et al. bring ML to verification planning itself, classifying regression test cases by ML algorithms and correlating with the amount of design change to predict which tests are relevant to a given RTL delta [Machine Learning Based Verification Planning Methodology, 2022].

The **AI/ML orchestration layer** is the newest and most aggressive. The 2026 papers are emphatic that the goal is no longer static ranking but *closed-loop* regression. Sharma et al.'s "Beyond Heuristics" argues that brute-force execution and heuristic-based resequencing "remain static, manual, and limited in adaptability" and proposes an AI/ML-driven methodology that predicts test utility and dynamically reprioritizes execution in response to evolving verification goals [Beyond Heuristics: AI/ML Driven Verification for Design Sign-off, 2026]. Chung et al.'s Smart Sanity Regression (SSR) unifies change-impact analysis, testset optimization, and failure triage into a single orchestrated workflow, explicitly positioning itself against the long TAT and high compute cost of full regression during active development [A Novel Fast Regression: An AI/ML Driven Automated Sanity Regression Flow, 2026]. Kashide et al. report similar themes for full SoC verification, where debug alone can consume over 50% of verification effort [A Novel AI-ML Regression Flow for SoC verification, 2025].

The **integration layer** connects regression to the rest of the enterprise. Graham argues that pure EDA metric management is insufficient because verification progress is entangled with defect tracking, requirements management, and source-code revision history, and that meaningful regression optimization requires connecting these enterprise applications to the MDV data stream [Connecting Enterprise Applications to Metric Driven Verification, 2014]. Meyer makes the same point at the system level: distributed hierarchical caches are an example of components whose verification state is spread across multiple regressions and is only meaningful when reassembled [So you think you have good stimulus, 2014].

## Pitfalls, gotchas, and where the field is heading

The biggest trap is optimizing the wrong thing. A regression that finishes faster but misses the bug that tape-out later finds has not been optimized — it has been degraded. The mitigation is to keep coverage and bug-find rate as the primary success metrics, not raw wall-clock time. Baek et al.'s heatmap-plus-ML approach is instructive: they achieved a 63% reduction in regression testing time *while* improving SDC coverage to 100% for timing exception paths, treating the two as joint objectives [Enhancing SDC Verification in SoCs, 2025].

A second trap is treating regression as a single-tier activity. Choi et al.'s 3-tiered framework is explicit that user-level sanity, block-level targeted, and chip-level integration regressions have different scopes and should be orchestrated by tier-specific agents coordinated through publish-subscribe bridges, rather than funneled through a single monolithic pipeline [A 3-tiered agentic AI Framework for Verification Regression, 2026]. Collapsing the tiers into one is the original sin of classical regression.

The trajectory is unmistakable. The papers from 2024 onward describe regression not as a batch job but as an autonomous system: closed-loop, agent-orchestrated, change-aware, and continuously learning from its own failures. The human verification manager is being repositioned as a supervisor of agents rather than the conductor of pipelines. Whether that fully materializes, the direction the corpus is pointing is clear.
