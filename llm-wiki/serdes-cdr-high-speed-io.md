# SerDes, CDR, and High-Speed I/O Verification

> *"Here is the kind of bug that ends careers: a multi-gigabit SerDes link passes every functional test you wrote, ships in volume, and then a customer reports a one-in-a-billion bit error under jitter conditions you never modeled. The classic DVCon paper by Sreenath, Kolipaka, and Shah opens by listing the standards that need a Clock-Data-Recovery (CDR) module — 'USB3.0/PCIE/MPHY/SATA/DP' — and points out that the block typically has 'Analog and Digital components where part of the circuit is Analog (typically the Equalizer, phase-interpolator, Samplers etc) and the CDR algorithm is implemented in digital RTL' [SERDES Rx CDR Verification using Jitter, SSC stimulus, 2014]. That split is the heart of the verification problem.

> Because the analog and digital halves are verified differently, in different tools, with different abstractions, the bug almost always lives in the seam. Schalick put it bluntly a decade ago for FPGA SerDes: 'logic simulation of SERDES-based designs is bogged down by long serial test sequences that can extend simulation times by 1-2 orders of magnitude,' and the IP is 'often incorporated via unfamiliar thirdparty IP blocks' that you cannot see inside [Addressing the verification challenge of SERDES-based FPGAs, 2011]. The picture is the same in 2025: Shim, Eom, and Kang argue that 'accurate and effective verification of digital logic requires the characteristics of analog front-end (AFE) components and channel to be included' [An Effective Digital Logic Verification Methodology of High Speed Interface IP, 2025].

> So how do you verify a block whose entire job is to lock onto a noisy analog signal at 112 Gbps, equalize it, recover a clock, and feed clean bits to digital logic? That is what this page is about — and the answer is a layered mix of behavioral AFE models, jitter-injected stimulus, UVM mixed-signal testbenches, IBIS-AMI channel sim, and elastic-buffer stress tests. Let's open the eye diagram."*

## What it is

A modern **SerDes** (Serializer/Deserializer) is a transceiver that moves bits over a differential pair at multi-gigabit rates. The receiver side has three jobs: **equalize** the incoming signal to undo channel loss, **recover the clock** from the data stream itself (there is no separate clock wire), and **retime** the data into a clean parallel bus. The transmit side adds the inverse: parallel-to-serial conversion, **feed-forward equalization (FFE)**, and a driver with pre-emphasis. Kumar and Herterich lay out the architecture: the digital PMA 'mangles the data and sends the appropriate word to the analog PMA (APMA),' which has 'a set of serializer units' architected to ease timing closure, with the parallel-to-serial data feeding 'the serializer, the Tx Feed Forward Equalizer (FFE) and the Tx IO driver' [Translating and Adapting to the "real" world, 2019]. The analog front-end (AFE) on receive typically includes a Passive Equalizer (PEQ), Continuous Time Linear Equalizer (CTLE), Decision Feedback Equalizer (DFE), phase shifter, and sense amplifiers [An Effective Digital Logic Verification Methodology, 2025].

**Clock-Data Recovery (CDR)** is the digital algorithm that closes the loop on the recovered clock. It samples the equalized data, compares phase against a reference, and adjusts the sampling point. Sreenath et al. describe the verification problem: 'As a first step, the architecture level analysis is done in tools like Matlab(Simulink)/Octave. This is followed by implementation of the analog and digital blocks. At some point, the implementation diverges from architecture' [SERDES Rx CDR Verification using Jitter, SSC stimulus, 2014]. CDR verification has to close that divergence — prove the RTL matches the architectural model and tolerates the jitter, spread-spectrum clocking (SSC), and ppm offsets the silicon will see.

The third piece is the **elastic buffer (EB)**, which sits in the receive path and absorbs the frequency difference between the recovered clock and the local reference clock. Velmail, Bose, and Lonkar explain its role: 'the PHY is also responsible for managing Clock Tolerance Compensation (CTC) between the far-end and receiver clock domains to ensure data-integrity in the Rx data-path,' and the EB does this with SKP (skip) ordered sets that get added or removed [Challenges, Complexities and Advanced Verification Techniques in Stress Testing of Elastic Buffer in High Speed SERDES IPs, 2019]. Get the EB math wrong and you overflow or starve the buffer.

## How it is used in practice

The workhorse methodology is a **UVM mixed-signal testbench with injected jitter and SSC stimulus**. Sreenath et al. present a SystemVerilog module that adds 'various types of jitter' to the stimulus, reusable 'across various protocol standards (IP designs),' with explicit sign-off criteria for jitter tolerance regressions run over 'around 100000 seeds' [SERDES Rx CDR Verification using Jitter, SSC stimulus, 2014]. The same stimulus stress-tests the EB because 'accurate modelling of the jitter, ppm offset and SSC allows testing of' the buffer behavior. Kumar and Herterich extend this to a full UVM-based SerDes mixed-signal verification flow that addresses 'mixed signal IPs like SerDes' challenges in improving verification content and reducing the time to market [Translating and Adapting to the "real" world, 2019].

For **elastic-buffer stress testing**, Velmail et al. lay out a comprehensive corner-case methodology: shift the UI (unit interval) profile of the recovered clock or write clock of the EB with respect to the local clock, monitor SKP flow through serial and pipe-interface monitors, plot read/write pointer differences, and correlate them with the time-period difference between read and write clocks [Challenges, Complexities and Advanced Verification Techniques, 2019]. For Gen2 (10 Gbps), 'on an average for every 40 data blocks, a SKP Ordered Set should be transmitted,' and SKP cannot be sent during training sets or data packets, which creates the worst-case scenarios the buffer must handle.

For **digital logic verification under realistic analog conditions**, Shim et al. propose a configurable AFE behavioral and channel model. Because 'the digital logic is responsible for important functions such as equalizer coefficients estimation and offset calibration (OC) to achieve target bit-error-rate (BER),' the digital logic verification needs an AFE model with realistic built-in offset, channel modeled as a 10-tap FIR filter, and a DFE that 'subtracts the product of the feedback data and the tap coefficient values found in the sign-sign least-meansquare (SSLMS) logic from the input' [An Effective Digital Logic Verification Methodology, 2025]. The configurability matters: previous flows relied on extracted behavioral models that had to be re-extracted every time the channel or AFE spec changed.

For **DDR/LPDDR PHY specifically**, Asif et al. tackle LPDDR5X PHY verification with DFE — the memory-side SerDes analog that has to hit 'a maximum data rate of 9600 mbps which can be coined as LPDDR5T(Turbo)' [The Road to Robustness: Addressing LPDDR5X PHY Verification Challenges with DFE, 2023]. And for **system-level SerDes validation**, Rathinavel argues for IBIS-AMI models as the bridge between RTL verification and PCB channel simulation, since 'as both complexity of the ICs and of the PCB increase to support these new solutions, it becomes very important to verify and estimate performance before' tapeout [Verification of High-Speed Links through IBIS-AMI Models, 2022].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The biggest pitfall is **simulation time**. Schalick's old observation still holds: serial test sequences 'extend simulation times by 1-2 orders of magnitude,' and the hierarchical protocols make it 'harder to thoroughly exercise internal logic' [Addressing the verification challenge of SERDES-based FPGAs, 2011]. The fix is partly methodological — Kaur, Narayanaswami, and Ho propose EASI2L, a specification format for 'automated block interface generation and verification' that catches omissions and misinterpretations at non-standard interfaces [EASI2L, 2016] — and partly a shift to higher-level stimulus models. Le and Andrews describe how 'graph-based stimulus modeling, as an alternative to constrained random generation, can be leveraged to apply certain bug hunting strategies' that improve defect discovery rate by 'one or more orders of magnitude' [Efficient Bug-Hunting Techniques Using Graph-Based Stimulus Models, 2016].

The second pitfall is **reference-model fidelity for multi-standard PHYs**. K N and Gandhi point out that 'building these TLM-based reference models becomes increasingly challenging when supporting multiple design standards — such as PCIe, USB, and DP within a single PHY,' and propose customizable real-time post-processing that exposes intermediate data-integrity results during the test rather than only after [Breaking the Wait, 2026]. Real-time feedback shortens the debug loop for a class of bug that is otherwise invisible until end-of-test.

The third pitfall is **safety verification under fault injection**. SerDes links increasingly appear in automotive and safety-critical contexts, and Dahir et al.'s Design-for-Verification work on multi-pipeline resource-shared designs is part of the same push to make SerDes-class blocks verifiable under safety constraints [Using HLS to improve Design-for-Verification, 2021]. Kaja et al. apply statistical, model-driven fault propagation analysis to RISC-V variants in the same safety context [A Statistical and Model-Driven Approach, 2024], and Garg and Sonavane propose random-sampling fault injection to make exhaustive injection tractable [Methodology for Efficient Fault Injection using Random Sampling, 2024].

The field is converging on three directions. First, **configurable behavioral AFE/channel models** so that digital logic verification does not require re-extracting analog models on every spec change [An Effective Digital Logic Verification Methodology, 2025]. Second, **statistical and ML-driven stimulus and coverage** — Shah et al. report 'improvements in transaction efficiency by 49 percent' using K-means++ clustering on stimuli inside UVM [AFCML: Accelerating the Functional Coverage through Machine Learning within a UVM Framework, 2025], and Bhatnagar and Fricano's PSS-driven interconnect-bus methodology extends naturally to SerDes links as a portable test source [Product Life Cycle of an Interconnect Bus, 2019]. Third, **tighter coupling between RTL verification and channel simulation** via IBIS-AMI, so that the SerDes that passed in RTL also passes in the PCB it ships on [Verification of High-Speed Links through IBIS-AMI Models, 2022]. The eye diagram closes when the analog and digital worlds agree — and that agreement is the deliverable.
