# RDC (Reset Domain Crossing) Verification

> CDC gets the headlines, but reset domain crossings are the bug that wakes the lab engineer at 3am. Two flops in the *same* clock domain, but reset by different asynchronous resets — that is all it takes. When one reset asserts asynchronously, the source flop's data can change underneath the destination flop and produce exactly the kind of metastability that conventional flows do not catch. Worse, RDC issues are "illusive": they appear as random silicon failures that are nearly impossible to reproduce and diagnose in the lab, and the chip might pass every simulation you throw at it before failing in the field. The DVCon corpus on RDC is younger and smaller than CDC's, and it is full of papers whose tone is half tutorial, half warning. Unlike CDC, there is little industry awareness of the danger, and that is precisely what makes it dangerous. So let's dig in.

## What it is and why simulation misses it

A **reset domain crossing (RDC)** arises whenever a signal crosses from a flop reset by one asynchronous reset to a flop reset by a different asynchronous reset, even when both flops share the same clock. The asynchronous assertion of the source reset can induce data changes that violate setup and hold timing at the destination flop, on top of inducing metastability — the same failure mode CDC protects against, but with resets as the cause instead of clocks [Comprehensive and Automated Static Tool Based Strategies for the Detection and Resolution of Reset Domain Crossings, 2017]. Because the bug requires a specific reset-assertion sequence to fire, running large numbers of simulation cycles in the hope of catching the alignment is "wasteful at best and impractical at worst" [Build Reliable and Efficient Reset Networks with a Comprehensive Reset Domain Crossing Verification Solution, 2020]. Static and formal methods are the only tractable answer.

Modern SoCs make this dramatically harder. High functionality, multiple interfaces, performance optimizations, and multi-mode operation split the chip into many reset domains, each receiving different combinations of primary reset sources, with dependencies that overlap at the crossings [Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure with Intelligent Reset Domain Crossings Detection, 2020]. A single chip can infer hundreds of combinational reset domains and tens of thousands of crossings, and the analysis has to model reset-assertion *sequence dependency* — which reset asserts first, and whether that ordering makes a crossing safe or hazardous. Static RDC tools can detect functional issues that escape both simulation and formal coverage precisely because they reason about reset ordering at the structural level [Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure with Intelligent Reset Domain Crossings Detection, 2020].

## Methodology, tooling, and the noise problem

The RDC flow looks structurally similar to CDC: structural analysis identifies crossings, classifies synchronizers or reset-protection structures, and produces violations that are either fixed in RTL or waived as false. The hard part is the volume. **Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure** reports that an initial RDC run without reset grouping or ordering information produced roughly 90,000 crossings; after applying reset grouping and explicit ordering for roughly 90 reset pairs, the inferred combinational reset domains dropped from many hundreds to 158 [Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure with Intelligent Reset Domain Crossings Detection, 2020]. The lesson is that constraint authoring — telling the tool which resets are dependent, ordered, or mutually exclusive — is where engineering effort actually goes, and a missing constraint can either hide a real bug or flood the report with noise.

The cleanest way to resolve an RDC issue is to avoid it: use synchronous resets, and the tool reports nothing [Comprehensive and Automated Static Tool Based Strategies for the Detection and Resolution of Reset Domain Crossings, 2017]. In real low-power designs that is rarely an option, so the alternatives are architectural modifications, specialized constraints, and dynamic waiver generation. **Build Reliable and Efficient Reset Networks** makes the case that RDC verification must be paired with a *structural* reset network design — constraints such as isolation-enable signals or reset-sequence ordering let the tool prove a crossing is valid where it would otherwise have to flag it [Build Reliable and Efficient Reset Networks with a Comprehensive Reset Domain Crossing Verification Solution, 2020]. The closing of the loop matters too: **The beginning of new norm** argues that RDC constraints, which encode assumptions about reset behavior, must themselves be signed off through functional simulation because a wrong assumption becomes a silicon failure [The beginning of new norm: CDC/RDC constraints signoff through functional simulation, 2024].

A particularly nasty structural complication is **set/reset flops**. When a flop has multiple asynchronous set/reset controls and its output is itself used as a reset further down the design, the question of which reset domain the flop belongs to stops being simple [Reset Domain Crossing for designs with set/reset flops, 2021]. **Bringing Reset Domains and Power Domains Together** shows that once you add UPF-described power elements, the interaction between asynchronous resets, clocks, and power domains becomes a three-body problem, and any design that incorporates UPF low-power elements is prone to erupt in metastability you did not expect [Bringing Reset Domains and Power Domains Together, 2021].

## Synergy with CDC, configurability, and ML assistance

Two themes recur across the recent RDC literature. The first is **synergy with CDC**. Tools like Questa RDC and Questa CDC share a common setup environment, so a single setup can be reviewed once rather than separately for each, and overlapping CDC/RDC paths can be deferred to whichever flow reviews them more cheaply — a real production design reported upwards of 5,000 RDC paths overlapping with CDC paths, all of which could be ignored during RDC analysis because the CDC team had already reviewed them [Resetting RDC Expectations, 2021]. Status-constraint management infrastructures then triage the large volume of results that configurable designs produce [Resetting RDC Expectations, 2021][Avoiding Confounding Configurations, 2022].

The second is the arrival of ML and AI in the constraint-authoring loop. **Reset Your Reset Domain Crossing (RDC) Verification with Machine Learning** points out that the early-stage challenges — setting up a baseline, refining setup for accurate results, grouping related violations into minimal accurate constraints, managing exceptions — are exactly the tasks ML is good at, and adds ML assistance to help work through early-stage issues and target smaller issues as a design matures [Reset Your Reset Domain Crossing (RDC) Verification with Machine Learning, 2022]. **Next-Generation CDC and RDC Closure** goes further: it uses an AI/ML utility (`CDC/RDC assist`) to generate a set of CDC/RDC constraints, then formally and functionally validates them through a protocol/constraint validation flow, with the goal of producing validated constraints without user intervention [Next-Generation CDC and RDC Closure, 2025].

Where the field is heading is convergence on three fronts: CDC and RDC sharing setup, tooling, and review; static, formal, and functional methods chained so that each catches what the others miss; and ML taking over the constraint-authoring grind that has historically consumed engineer-hours. **Left Shift Mechanism to Mitigate Gate Level Asynchronous Design Challenges** captures the practical urgency — gate-level RDC signoff is hard because of missing IP collateral, weak clock-reset architectural understanding in the back-end domain, waiver-import issues, and schedule pressure, but it is unavoidable for glitch-free reset across power states [Left Shift Mechanism to Mitigate Gate Level Asynchronous Design Challenges, 2022]. The trajectory is toward RDC becoming a first-class, ML-assisted, left-shifted discipline rather than the afterthought it has historically been.

## Grounded in these DVCon papers

- **Achieving Faster Reset Verification Closure with Intelligent Reset Domain Crossings Detection** (2020, DVCon Europe) — Milanpreet Kaur and Sulabh Kumar Khare. Emphasizes reset-assertion sequence dependency and shows how reset grouping and ordering collapse the violation count.
- **Comprehensive and Automated Static Tool Based Strategies for the Detection and Resolution of Reset Domain Crossings** (2017, DVCon US) — Yossi Mirsky. A foundational static-tool methodology for RDC detection and resolution strategies.
- **Build Reliable and Efficient Reset Networks with a Comprehensive Reset Domain Crossing Verification Solution** (2020, DVCon Europe) — Wanggen Shi, Yuxin You and Kurt Takara. Argues for a structural reset-network design paired with RDC verification.
- **Resetting RDC Expectations: A Systematic Approach to Verifying Complex Configurable Designs** (2021, DVCon Europe) — Eamonn Quigley, Jonathan Niven and Mark Handover. Leverages CDC/RDC synergies and status-constraint management for configurable designs.
- **Reset Domain Crossing for designs with set/reset flops** (2021, DVCon US) — Abdul Moyeen and Inayat Ali. Addresses the structural complications of multi-reset set/reset flops.
- **The beginning of new norm: CDC/RDC constraints signoff through functional simulation** (2024, DVCon US) — Suhas D S, Ponsankar Arumugam and Deepmala Sachan. Argues RDC constraints must be signed off through functional simulation to catch wrong assumptions.
- **Left Shift Mechanism to Mitigate Gate Level Asynchronous Design Challenges** (2022, DVCon US) — Rohit Kumar Sinha and Kavya Kotha. A left-shift methodology for gate-level CDC/RDC signoff under collateral and schedule pressure.
- **Techniques to identify reset metastability issues due to soft resets** (2023, DVCon India) — Reetika and Sulabh Kumar Khare. Sign-off methodology for metastability from asynchronous and soft reset interactions.
- **Reset Your Reset Domain Crossing (RDC) Verification with Machine Learning** (2022, DVCon Europe) — Mark Handover. Adds ML assistance to baseline setup, refinement, constraint authoring, and exception management.
- **A Specification-Driven Methodology for the Design and Verification of Reset Domain Crossing Logic** (2018, DVCon US) — Priya Viswanathan, Kurt Takara and Chris Kwok. A three-step requirements-based methodology for RDC design and verification.
- **Next-Generation CDC and RDC Closure: An AI/ML-Driven Approach for Automated and Validated Constraint** (2025, DVCon India) — Abdul Moyeen and Ravi Pathak. An AI/ML-assisted CDC/RDC constraint generation and formal/functional validation flow.
- **Avoiding Confounding Configurations: An RDC Methodology for Configurable Designs** (2022, DVCon US) — Eamonn Quigley, Jonathan Niven and Kurt Takara. RDC verification approach targeted at the configurability challenges of IP and subsystem development.
- **Bringing Reset Domains and Power Domains Together -Set/Reset Flops Augmenting Complexities in Power-Aware RDC Verification** (2021, DVCon Europe) — Manish Bhati, Manjunatha Srinivas and Abdul Moyeen. Examines the three-body problem of resets, clocks, and UPF power domains.
- **Evolution of CDC recipe: Learning through real case studies and methodology improvements** (2021, DVCon US) — Amit Kulkarni, Suhas DS and Deepmala Sachan. Joint CDC/RDC verification lessons from a graphics-chip program.
