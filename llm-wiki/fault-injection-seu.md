# Fault Injection and Single-Event Upset Verification

> *"Take an automotive RISC-V core with 10,000 fault locations running a million cycles. There are 10 to the 10th possible single stuck-at faults you could inject. Brute-force that and your campaign never finishes — and ISO 26262 politely asks you to prove diagnostic coverage anyway. The escape hatch is statistical fault injection: sample a few tens of thousands of faults at random, measure the detected fraction, and bound the error with a confidence interval. It works, but the moment you ship an ASIL-D chip you owe the assessor an explanation for every fault that your safety mechanism failed to detect — and there will be thousands of them. That is where fault injection stops being a simulation exercise and becomes a triage problem: which undetected faults are dangerous, which are latent, which are simply untestable by design? The answers decide whether your chip ships. The good news, as a decade of DVCon papers from Infineon, NXP, Samsung, and Siemens shows, is that the toolchain for answering those questions — formal, emulation, hybrid virtualization — has finally caught up with the size of the problem. Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**Fault injection** is the methodology of deliberately introducing hardware faults — stuck-at-0, stuck-at-1, transient bit-flips (single-event upsets), and intermittent faults — into a model of the design to verify that safety mechanisms detect them and drive the system safe inside the **fault tolerant time interval (FTTI)**. ISO 26262 recommends it for ASIL C/D devices to measure **diagnostic coverage (DC)**, the fraction of faults observed or diagnosed by the safety mechanism [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019]. The candidate space is enormous: with `N` fault locations, there are `2N` possible single stuck-at faults before you even consider the time dimension, so a campaign is necessarily a sampling exercise [A Statistical and Model-Driven Approach for Comprehensive Fault Propagation Analysis of RISC-V Variants, 2024].

The core abstraction is the **fault campaign**. A fault list is compiled (often as a FuSa database), each candidate fault is injected into a golden-run waveform, and the result is classified — detected by the safety mechanism, observed functionally, latent, or dangerous. Injection targets span abstraction levels: reusing the DV environment in RTL simulation, saboteurs and mutants injected into the hardware description, Verilog-VPI-based frameworks, or microarchitectural simulators enhanced with injection hooks (MARSS-MaFIN, Gem5-GeFIN) [A Statistical and Model-Driven Approach for Comprehensive Fault Propagation Analysis of RISC-V Variants, 2024]. Tang, Mizuki and Norcross push it up further still, modeling an entire LIDAR system in Simulink and injecting faults at the system level, including the safety mechanisms in the model itself [SYSTEM LEVEL FAULT INJECTION SIMULATION USING SIMULINK, 2017].

## How it's used in practice

The standard approach is **Statistical Fault Injection (SFI)** plus triage. Kaja, Gerlin and Yun give the canonical math: pick a random subset of faults, derive sample coverage, then bound the margin of error and confidence interval — for a 10¹⁰-space design, ~23,784 injections hit 99.8% confidence at 1% error [A Statistical and Model-Driven Approach for Comprehensive Fault Propagation Analysis of RISC-V Variants, 2024]. Uddagiri et al. applied this to ASIL-D BMS components, reusing the DV environment to inject permanent and transient faults and evaluate the diagnostic and fault-reaction time intervals against the FMEDA [Fault Injection Strategy to Validate ASIL-D Requirements of BMS Components, 2022]. For automotive NPUs the runtime is brutal — Likhopoy et al. document the data-management and runtime optimizations needed to drive an internal NPU campaign to certification on an emulator with SW-based safety mechanisms [Functional Safety Workflow of Internal IP (NPU) Within Large Automotive IC, 2024].

**Emulation and hybrid platforms** are where the throughput comes from. Nabil, Saied and Makram's hybrid verification interfaces an RTL emulator running peripheral IP to a host running a virtualized CPU through a transaction-level interface, achieving 10× speedup over full emulation and 1000× over RTL sim at 0.12% of resources; their NVDLA case study revealed that neural accelerators show only moderate vulnerability to single-point faults (~10% accuracy loss) but "become increasingly susceptible with multiple simultaneous faults injected (95% accuracy loss with 32 faults)" — a finding invisible to single-fault campaigns [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025]. Bagbaba, Da Silva and Sauer cross-check the fault list against ATPG stuck-at coverage to lift the **tool confidence level (TCL)** of the FI simulator itself, an ISO 26262 requirement that is easy to overlook [Improving the Confidence Level in Functional Safety Simulation Tools for ISO 26262, 2018].

**Formal** enters both to prune and to prove. Ahuja et al. use formal to classify unobserved faults as untestable-by-design, closing the diagnostic-coverage gap without brute force [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019]; Yeung, Smith and Ayari add static analysis to shrink the fault list before injection, then formal fault injection to maximize observability of safety failures [Whose Fault Is It Formally?, 2018]. Traskov, Ehrenberg and Loitz apply the same idea to a Triple Modular Redundancy element and its majority voter — small gate counts, ideal for formal — walking through fault population reduction, injection, checking, classification, and metrics [Fault Proof: Using Formal Techniques for Safety Verification and Fault Analysis, 2016]. The newest twist is **formal-driven ATPG for software-based self-test (SBST)**: Al Halabi, Kaja and Ecker combine formal verification with fault simulation to auto-generate test programs that the processor runs to test itself, in the field as well as in production [Automatic Test Pattern Generation Using Formal Verification and Fault Injection Methods, 2025].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The hardest problem is the **unclassified fault**. When a campaign finishes, the faults that are unobserved functionally and undetected by the safety mechanism (the "UU" class) still have to be reclassified before diagnostic coverage can be claimed with confidence. Rajanedi and Ravindra argue that simulation-based **fault barrier analysis** has real advantages over conventional formal methods for specifically resolving UU faults — it gives an evidence trail an assessor can follow [Decoding the Unknown: A Synergy of Formal and Simulation Methods for Unclassified Faults, 2025]. Baudoin and Garg put their finger on the deeper issue: without **elementary subpart (ESP) granularity**, coverage justification — "ensuring that safety mechanisms comprehensively protect safety-relevant elements" — is wishful thinking, and architectural weaknesses surface too late, only in the RTL campaign [Breaking Down Failures, 2026].

The second pitfall is **fault-list fidelity and model drift**. Different injection mechanisms cover different fault types — permanent SA0/SA1 cleanly, transients and bit-flips less so — so a campaign that only models stuck-ats can certify a chip that still dies to a single-event upset [Functional Safety Workflow of Internal IP (NPU) Within Large Automotive IC, 2024]. SFI's statistical comfort also rests on a normal-distribution hypothesis; rare but dangerous corner faults can sit in the tail [A Statistical and Model-Driven Approach for Comprehensive Fault Propagation Analysis of RISC-V Variants, 2024].

The field is converging on **hybrid, formal-assisted, emulation-accelerated campaigns with quantified confidence**. The trajectory runs from simulation-only SFI toward formal pre-classification of untestable faults [Formal Assisted Fault Campaign for ISO26262 Certification, 2019], from RTL-only campaigns toward emulation and emulation-plus-virtualization for software safety mechanisms [Pre-Silicon Verification of Software Safety Mechanisms, 2025], and from gate-level-only analysis toward RTL-granularity fault lists with formal ATPG feeding SBST programs that travel with the chip for life [Automatic Test Pattern Generation Using Formal Verification and Fault Injection Methods, 2025]. The fault campaign isn't going away — but it is becoming faster, more formal, and far more defensible.
