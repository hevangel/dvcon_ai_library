# OSVVM — Open Source VHDL Verification Methodology

> *"UVM gets all the press, but the VHDL world has its own answer, and it is older and arguably cleaner. **OSVVM** — Open Source VHDL Verification Methodology — is a set of VHDL packages built by Jim Lewis of SynthWorks since the early 2010s that brings functional coverage, randomization, intelligent test generation, and a unified alert log to a language that already had strong typing, generics, and protected types. Lewis's 2014 DVCon Europe pitch was characteristically blunt: OSVVM's Intelligent Coverage randomization 'is a step ahead of other verification methodologies, such as SystemVerilog's UVM.' That is a bold claim, but the structural argument is real — VHDL teams who work on aerospace, automotive, and FPGA flows never adopted SystemVerilog, and they needed a methodology that spoke their language and ran on their tools. Where UVM is 357 classes and 1037 methods, OSVVM is a small library of packages that does the same job in plain VHDL. Let's look at what makes it different."*

## What it is

OSVVM is implemented as a free, open-source VHDL library built around three core packages: **CoveragePkg** for functional coverage and Intelligent Coverage randomization, **RandomPkg** for plain randomization, and **AlertLogPkg** for coordinated error reporting. The defining design choice is that OSVVM is *package-based*, not *class-based* like UVM. There is no `uvm_component` hierarchy and no phasing; the testbench is structured VHDL with shared variables holding coverage and alert state [OSVVM: Advanced Verification for VHDL, 2014]. Lewis argues this is what makes OSVVM lighter to learn than UVM, which Sutherland and Fitzpatrick measure at "357 classes, 1037 methods and 374 macros" — and which they themselves subset down to a small core in their UVM-Light proposal [UVM-Light A Subset of UVM for Rapid Adoption, 2015].

The signature feature is **Intelligent Coverage**. Constrained random in SystemVerilog separates the constraint solver from the functional coverage model and hopes the two converge. OSVVM inverts this: instead of a constraint solver it randomizes *across coverage holes* — given N randomizations, the goal is to generate N unique coverage hits. Lewis demonstrates this with a UART example where nominal, parity-error, stop-error, and break-error test cases are weighted by `AddBins(70, GenBin(0))` and the test loop runs `while not Bin1.IsCovered loop`, drawing `iSequence := Bin1.RandCovPoint` each iteration [OSVVM: Advanced Verification for VHDL, 2014]. As he notes, "constrained random tests are not the strong point of OSVVM" — there is no solver, so coverage closure becomes the engine instead.

## How it's used in practice

OSVVM's natural home is **VHDL FPGA flows** where SystemVerilog was never an option. Lewis's follow-up paper extends the methodology to error reporting: the **AlertLogPkg** provides a data structure and procedures that pull every testbench error into a common format and produce a single pass/fail indication at end of test, replacing ad-hoc `report` statements scattered through the testbench [OSVVM and Error Reporting, 2015]. For mixed-language projects, OSVVM plays well with SystemVerilog. Mukherjee, Verma, and Kakkar lay out a comprehensive SystemVerilog-VHDL mixed-language methodology at Mentor Graphics, and Singh and Verma at Infineon describe plugging functional coverage methodology gaps in mixed SystemC/VHDL environments using UCIS as the interchange format [COMPREHENSIVE SYSTEMVERILOG-SYSTEMC-VHDL MIXED-LANGUAGE DESIGN METHODOLOGY, 2010; Plugging the Holes: SystemC and VHDL Functional Coverage Methodology, 2011].

Coverage integration with corporate tooling is where OSVVM teams spend real time. Eickhoff, DeBole, and Ekwueme-Okoli at IBM describe an assertion-based approach to VHDL functional coverage in mixed cycle/event-driven simulation: they extend VHDL to collect coverage, then "use a script to convert the combined simulation functional coverage statistics into an XML format" that maps onto covergroups before UCIS conversion, so corporate dashboards and tooling that speak UCIS see OSVVM coverage natively [An Assertion Based Approach to Implement VHDL Functional Coverage, 2014]. Brosens argues from the productivity side that the same VHDL codebase benefits from a "software tool that helps you to create and explore code in a more efficient way," including functional-safety-grade VHDL where OSVVM is the verification backbone [Boost your productivity in FPGA & ASIC design and verification, 2022].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The most obvious pitfall is **no constraint solver**. OSVVM explicitly trades constrained-random richness for the coverage-driven loop, but that means complex legal-input constraints still need hand-coded case statements or external randomization. Lewis admits as much: "it is up to the test coder to ensure that a balance of vectors are generated" — and even with SystemVerilog's solver, closure is hard [OSVVM: Advanced Verification for VHDL, 2014]. A second pitfall is tooling: VHDL 2008 is required for protected types and generic packages, which not all FPGA toolchains supported well until recently. Lemiengre and Eeckhaut's VHDL 2018 survey shows the standard catching up with "enhanced protected and generic types" and interfaces that "enable designers to build the next generation of verification libraries" — many of which OSVVM now depends on [VHDL 2018: New and Noteworthy, 2018]. Performance is a third: Strähle's AMS-in-UVM paper at Infineon reports reusing behavior VHDL models for analog blocks where SystemVerilog UVM was the surrounding environment, and OSVVM-aware VHDL has to coexist with that mixed-language soup [AMS Verification in a UVM Environment, 2016].

The trajectory is OSVVM-as-VHDL-citizen alongside other methodologies rather than as a UVM rival. Poppen, Trunzer, and Oetjens at Bosch and OFFIS show how a company's proprietary VHDL methodology gets mapped onto "standard concepts of UVM" so that mixed teams can share vocabulary while keeping their tooling [Connecting a Company's Verification Methodology to Standard Concepts of UVM, 2014]. Simulation-acceleration work at PMC (Cadence) demonstrates that mixed-language SystemVerilog/Verilog/VHDL designs with UVM-e stimulus can be ported to hardware-assisted verification with 67x speedup — and OSVVM testbenches tag along for the ride [A Methodology to Port a Complex Multi-Language Design and Testbench for Simulation Acceleration, 2015]. With VHDL 2018 landing the language features OSVVM needs and Lewis continuing to ship packages, OSVVM remains the de-facto verification methodology for the VHDL half of the industry.
