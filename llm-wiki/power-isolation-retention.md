# Power Isolation, Retention, and Level Shifters

> *"You can't just turn a chip off. When you power-gate a domain, the logic inside goes to X — every flip-flop loses its mind, every output floats, every signal that crosses the boundary becomes a hazard. So the silicon team adds three small but expensive pieces of plumbing: isolation cells that clamp the outputs to a known value, retention registers that keep critical state alive on a backup rail, and level shifters that translate between domains running at different voltages. They sound trivial. They are not. Qualcomm titled a 2023 paper 'Wrong clamps can kill your chip!!' for a reason — a single isolation cell clamped to the wrong value, or a retention register that loses state because its save signal glitched, can brick a part that took three years to tape out. So how do you verify these tiny cells that sit between you and a silicon respin? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

A modern low-power SoC is partitioned into **power domains** — regions that can be switched off or scaled independently to save energy — and the boundaries between those domains need special cells to keep the chip sane. **Isolation cells** detach signals crossing from a power-gated domain to an always-on domain, clamping them to a safe 0 or 1 so the receiving logic never sees a floating value; **retention registers** preserve the state of key flip-flops while the rest of the block is powered down, then restore it on wake-up; and **level shifters** step signals up or down when two always-on domains run at different voltages [An Automated Validation Framework for Power Management and Data Retention Logic Kits of Standard Cell Library, 2021]. All three are described in the **Unified Power Format (UPF, IEEE 1801)**, the standard that lets designers specify power intent separately from the RTL.

These cells look like simple library elements, but their behavior depends on power-supply attributes that live in the Liberty views — `related_power_pin`, `related_ground_pin`, `power_down_function` — and on the UPF strategies that say where to insert them [Supply network connectivity: An imperative part in low power gate-level verification, 2019]. Isolation cells only make sense at the boundary of a power-gated domain; retention cells only make sense inside one. The UPF library commands introduced in 2.1 finally let designers describe the special power-management cells and hard macros in UPF itself, instead of forcing them to live partly in UPF and partly in Liberty [The UPF 2.1 library commands: Truly unifying the power specification formats, 2015]. The complexity is real: a single design can have isolation, retention, level shifter, combiner-enabled level shifter, and repeater cells, each with their own truth table and supply sensitivities [An Automated Validation Framework for Power Management and Data Retention Logic Kits of Standard Cell Library, 2021].

## How it's used in practice

The flow runs in two complementary modes: **static checking** that asks "are the right cells in the right places?" and **dynamic simulation** that asks "do they behave correctly when power toggles?". Static tools like MVRC take the UPF Power State Table (PST) as the golden reference and verify that isolation and level shifter requirements are met at every domain crossing [A Systematic Approach to Power State Table (PST) Debugging, 2013]. But static checking can't verify that the **clamp value** is correct — an isolation cell clamped to 1 where the spec wanted 0 will pass static and fail in silicon. Arora et al. argue that dynamic power-aware simulation is the only way to catch wrong clamps, and that with today's SoC complexity, exhaustively covering every clamp value demands many test scenarios [Wrong clamps can kill your chip!!….find them early, 2023].

Library teams have to validate these cells before they ever ship. Kamath et al. at Samsung built an automated framework that infers a unique instance of every power-management and data-retention cell in a single netlist — isolation cells inferred from a power-gated stimulus block to an always-on monitor, retention cells inside a "retention island," level shifters at every voltage crossing — achieving 100% logical cell coverage by combining always-on and power-gated SFR blocks at different voltages [An Automated Validation Framework for Power Management and Data Retention Logic Kits of Standard Cell Library, 2021]. At the SoC level, path-based UPF strategies (introduced in UPF 3.0/3.1) let designers say exactly which source-to-sink paths need isolation or level shifting, replacing the older port-based approach that would over-place isolation cells whenever a port fanned out to multiple domains [Path-based UPF Strategies: Optimally Manage Power on your Designs, 2022]. And retention optimization is now its own discipline: Kalwani and Silva introduce "Retention Sufficiency" — a methodology for identifying which state elements genuinely need retention, so designers stop over-retaining every register in a collapsible domain and paying the area and leakage tax [Retention Sufficiency Validation for Optimizing State Retention Cells in Low Power Design, 2024].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The deepest pitfall is that **low-power cells create new CDC hazards**. Insert an isolation cell on a clock path and you've built combinational logic in the clock path — a glitch on the isolation enable can metastabilize the receiving flop, and an isolation enable from an asynchronous domain can violate setup-hold at the destination [Advanced Techniques to Accomplish Power Aware CDC Verification, 2018]. The same applies to retention: designers used to believe the save/restore protocol protected retention logic from metastability, but Handover et al. show that CDC crossings to and from retention cells can still break, and that DVFS — which moves voltage domains in and out of operation — multiplies the number of asynchronous clocks and voltage-domain-crossing (VDC) paths you have to analyze [Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015]. Sinha et al. catalog three specific failure modes: glitches on clock paths from isolation logic, new CDC paths from isolation enables, and broken CDC synchronizers where isolation logic adds combinational logic to a previously clean path [Advanced Techniques to Accomplish Power Aware CDC Verification, 2018].

The second pitfall is **gate-level simulation corruption semantics**. When the supply network is incomplete or the Liberty attributes are wrong, the simulator's corruption behavior — what goes to X and when — depends on those power attributes being correctly defined, and a missing connection can either hide a bug or fabricate one [Supply network connectivity: An imperative part in low power gate-level verification, 2019]. Multi-rail macros like PLLs, SERDES, and memories compound this: a macro with more than one supply rail has to be modeled with power-aware Verilog views, and integrating those into a power-aware flow is its own research problem [Tackling the challenge of simulating multi-rail macros in a power aware flow, 2014]. The field is heading toward RTL-level power-aware CDC analysis — running the checks earlier, doing what-if analysis on power architectures before gate-level modifications become expensive, and treating isolation/retention/level-shifter correctness as a first-class CDC concern rather than an afterthought [Power Aware CDC Verification at RTL for Faster SoC Verification Closure, 2014] [Next-generation Power Aware CDC Verification -What have we learned?, 2015].
