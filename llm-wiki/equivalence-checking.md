# Equivalence Checking (LEC / SEC)

> *Back in 1995, the Pentium FDIV bug cost Intel $475 million — a floating-point divide error that slipped through simulation because nobody thought to test those exact operands. That single incident is a big reason equivalence checking exists. The idea is seductively simple: instead of writing test vectors and hoping you have covered enough, you mathematically prove that two representations of a design — your golden RTL and the synthesized netlist, or your RTL and a C++ reference model — produce identical outputs for every possible input. No stimulus, no coverage holes, just a proof. In practice it is never that clean: capacity blows up, state mappings get messy, and somebody still has to write the constraints. So how did the industry make it routine?*

## Combinational vs sequential: two flavors of equivalence

There are two major flavors of logic equivalence checking, and the choice between them is usually dictated by design size and whether state points can be mapped one-to-one. **Combinational Equivalence Checking (CEC)** proves equivalence for combinational logic cones between matched state points; it is efficient and scales well, but only works when every flip-flop in the RTL has a corresponding flip-flop in the implementation [Using Model Checking to Prove Constraints of Combinational Equivalence Checking, 2010]. At AMD, where design size rules out the alternative, CEC is the tool of choice precisely because synthesis rules guarantee that 1-to-1 state mapping [Using Model Checking to Prove Constraints of Combinational Equivalence Checking, 2010].

**Sequential Equivalence Checking (SEC)**, by contrast, traverses the product finite state machine of the two designs, so equivalence of intermediate state points is not required. This lets SEC prove functional equivalence across levels of sequential and data abstraction — verifying that micro-architectural optimizations for power, timing, and area do not introduce functional side effects [Efficient and Exhaustive Floating Point Verification Using Sequential Equivalence Checking, 2017]. The tradeoff is that SEC is far more sensitive to design size than CEC. Both rest on similar proof engines (BDDs or SAT solvers), but SEC has to reason about state elements, which makes it the more powerful but costlier option [Using Model Checking to Prove Constraints of Combinational Equivalence Checking, 2010].

## Where equivalence checking earns its keep

The canonical SEC use case is **floating-point verification**. Exhaustive simulation of an FPU is impossible — the operand space is astronomically large — so the industry has converged on proving equivalence of FADD, FMUL, FMA, FDIV, and FSQRT blocks against C/C++/SystemC reference models. ARM and Mentor partnered to formally verify the hardest FP blocks, including FDIV and FSQRT, in high-end Cortex A-class CPUs [Efficient and Exhaustive Floating Point Verification Using Sequential Equivalence Checking, 2017]. The same C-RTL equivalence approach extends to deep-learning instructions on GPUs [Formal Verification on Deep Learning Instructions of GPU, 2018].

Clock-gating verification is another SEC stronghold. NVIDIA built an automated SEC "App" for chip-wide clock-gating verification that takes advantage of symmetry to achieve deep or full proofs; across multiple projects it found 32 clock-gating bugs, half of which simulation would have caught only as post-silicon escapes [A Formal Verification App Towards Efficient, Chip-Wide Clock Gating Verification, 2014]. At AMD, the demand for higher performance within a power budget forced custom structures throughout the microprocessor core, defeating traditional equivalence methods and requiring an exhaustive equivalence-checking framework for 100% coverage at reasonable runtime [Exhaustive Equivalence Checking on AMD's Next-generation Microprocessor Core, 2011]. Low-power design adds yet another dimension: the power-intent file (CPF or IEEE 1801 UPF) becomes part of the specification, and simulation-to-implementation equivalence checking is needed to confirm every tool in the flow interpreted the intent the same way [The Case for Low-Power Simulation-to-Implementation Equivalence Checking, 2012; What's New in IEEE 1801 and Why?, 2025]. State-retention cells in power-aware designs can even be optimized by validating which registers truly need retention through SEC-based "retention sufficiency" analysis [Retention Sufficiency Validation for Optimizing State Retention Cells in Low Power Design, 2024].

## Scaling it: abstractions, constraints, and checking the checkers

Capacity is the recurring enemy. GPU architectures with scalable blocks that can be enabled or disabled produce an explosion of configurations that traditional verification cannot enumerate; a formal equivalence approach that reasons over the full design — including disabled components — sidesteps the combinatorial blowup [Novel Formal Equivalence Approach to Verify Scalable Architecture in GPU, 2025]. Parameterized modules present a similar bottleneck, since each parameterization is effectively a new RTL, and a methodology that avoids testing every configuration separately is essential [Breaking the Formal Verification Bottleneck, 2024]. When a bug does exist, equality-saturation rewriting can automatically bridge the gap between specification and implementation, decomposing the proof or even proposing a minimal fix [Automating Datapath Verification and Bug Correction via Equality Saturation, 2025].

A humbling thread in recent work asks: who checks the checkers? Because equivalence checkers (ECs) authorize design signoff, ridding them of bugs is a top priority. Randomized fuzzing of SystemC-to-RTL ECs has uncovered multiple unsoundness bugs — where the EC claimed equivalence incorrectly — in major commercial tools [Who checks the checkers?, 2024]. Reducing the manual burden on designers, through better constraint harvesting and property-specification-language assumptions, is the other practical lever [Designers Work Less with Quality Formal Equivalence Checking, 2010]. And where timing closure forces gate-level simulation, GLS complements rather than replaces equivalence checks, catching issues that synthesis and LEC miss [HISIG — An Efficient Gate Level Simulation Flow for a Hard IP Inside a Soft IP, 2023].

## Grounded in these DVCon papers

- **Efficient and Exhaustive Floating Point Verification Using Sequential Equivalence Checking** (2017, DVCon US) — Travis W. Pouarz and Vaibhav Agrawal. The canonical SEC case study: proving FPU blocks (FMUL, FMA, FDIV, FSQRT) against reference models.
- **Using Model Checking to Prove Constraints of Combinational Equivalence Checking** (2010, DVCon US) — Xiushan Feng, Joseph Gutierrez and Mel Pratt. Distills CEC vs SEC tradeoffs and why AMD standardizes on CEC with model-checked constraints.
- **Exhaustive Equivalence Checking on AMD's Next-generation Microprocessor Core** (2011, DVCon US) — Baosheng Wang, Brian McMinn and Borhan Roohipour. Advanced EC framework for custom microprocessor structures that defeat traditional methods.
- **A Formal Verification App Towards Efficient, Chip-Wide Clock Gating Verification** (2014, DVCon US) — Prosenjit Chatterjee, Scott Fields and Syed Suhaib. Automated SEC app that found 32 clock-gating bugs across NVIDIA projects.
- **The Case for Low-Power Simulation-to-Implementation Equivalence Checking** (2012, DVCon US) — Himanshu Bhatt, John Decker and Hiral Desai. Why power-aware design needs equivalence checking anchored in the UPF/CPF power-intent file.
- **Novel Formal Equivalence Approach to Verify Scalable Architecture in GPU** (2025, DVCon India) — Sanjana Jain and Ipshita Tripathi. Formal equivalence over the full scalable GPU design including disabled components.
- **Retention Sufficiency Validation for Optimizing State Retention Cells in Low Power Design** (2024, DVCon Europe) — Nitesh Kalwani and Mateus Silva. SEC-based optimization of which registers truly need retention in power-aware designs.
- **Formal Verification on Deep Learning Instructions of GPU** (2018, DVCon US) — Jian (Jeffrey) Wang and Jia Zhu. C-RTL equivalence checking for deep-learning instructions with capacity optimizations.
- **Designers Work Less with Quality Formal Equivalence Checking** (2010, DVCon US) — Orly Cohen, Moran Gordon and Michael Lifshits. Reducing designer effort in SAT-based FEC with SVA assumptions for the environment.
- **What's New in IEEE 1801 and Why?** (2025, DVCon US) — Progyna Khondkar. How evolving UPF standards complicate power-intent interpretation that equivalence checking must reconcile.
- **HISIG — An Efficient Gate Level Simulation Flow for a Hard IP Inside a Soft IP** (2023, DVCon India) — Ishwar Ganiger, Vishal Dalal and Johannes Grinschgl. GLS complementing equivalence checks where timing closure is done at IP level.
- **Automating Datapath Verification and Bug Correction via Equality Saturation** (2025, DVCon US) — Emiliano Morini, Samuel Coward and Theo Drane. Word-level RTL rewriting to decompose equivalence proofs and propose minimal fixes.
- **Breaking the Formal Verification Bottleneck: Faster and More Comprehensive Testing of Parameterized Modules** (2024, DVCon US) — Menachem Rappaport, Ariel Ansbacher and Elchanan Rappaport. Avoiding per-configuration blowup when verifying parameterized SoC designs.
- **Who checks the checkers? Automatically finding bugs in C-to-RTL formal equivalence checkers** (2024, DVCon Europe) — Michalis Pardalos, Alastair F. Donaldson and Emiliano Morini. Fuzzing commercial ECs to expose unsoundness and incompleteness bugs.
