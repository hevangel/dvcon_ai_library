# PSS — Portable Stimulus Standard

> *Write your stimulus once, run it on simulation, emulation, and post-silicon. That is the PSS pitch, and it sounds too good to be true because, well, it took the industry a decade to make it real. The pain it attacks is universal: you have an SoC with a thousand register combinations, half a dozen verification engines, and a post-silicon lab that re-uses maybe 10% of what pre-silicon produced. Accellera's **Portable Stimulus Standard (PSS)** promises a single declarative model of *test intent* that a tool lowers into concrete tests for each platform. The DVCon papers on PSS are the long, honest record of teams trying, succeeding, struggling, and ultimately learning where the abstraction pays off and where it leaks. Let's dig in.*

## What PSS actually is

PSS is a **declarative domain-specific language** for capturing *verification intent* at a level above any single execution platform [Bridge the Portable Test and Stimulus to UVM Simulation Environment, 2018]. Instead of writing a UVM sequence, an emulation test, and a lab script for the same scenario, you describe the scenario once — the components, the data flow, the constraints on legal configurations — and a PSS tool **synthesizes** concrete tests for whatever target you need. The model is heavily **constraint-based**, capturing value relationships that define the legal test space, and exposes two mechanisms for connecting to target-specific code: procedure calls and **target templates**, both wrapped in `exec` blocks [Designing PSS Environment Integration for Maximum Reuse, 2020].

The standard is explicit that intent and realization are separate concerns. A PSS model describes *what* a test should do; the realization layer — implemented per target — describes *how* to drive it on a UVM testbench, an emulator, or bare-metal C [There and Back Again, 2026]. That separation is what makes portability possible, but it is also where most adoption pain lives, because each target needs its own realization APIs and the integration between model and environment is easy to get wrong [Designing PSS Environment Integration for Maximum Reuse, 2020]. The promise is that one well-structured model can produce UVM tests, C-based Linux and bare-metal tests, and post-silicon ATE tests from the same source [There and Back Again, 2026][Accelerating Device Sign-off, 2025].

## Where PSS earns its keep in practice

The earliest and most convincing wins were on **system-level scenarios** that are miserable to write as directed tests. Cache coherency and deadlock avoidance on multi-core SoCs were a flagship application: PSS tools like **Perspec** auto-generate self-checking scenarios that stress the coherency protocol and explore deadlocks far more thoroughly than hand-written tests [Coherency Verification & Deadlock Detection Using Perspec, 2019]. The same model-driven generation handles product life cycles — Performance modeling, DV, and post-silicon validation of an interconnect bus fabric can all be driven from a generic PSS traffic generator [Product Life Cycle of an Interconnect Bus, 2019]. Where the standard has matured, PSS coverage (added in the 1.0a release) lets you grade generated stimulus *before* it runs, giving a coverage-driven-verification feel inside the PSS model itself [Benefits of PSS coverage at SOC, 2019].

The other big payoff is **vertical and cross-platform reuse**. A single constrained-random specification can be re-targeted across IP, subsystem, and SoC, and across simulation, emulation, and silicon — a key advantage as designs grow into chiplets and across market segments [Scalable Functional Verification using Portable Stimulus Standard, 2024]. Bridging PSS to an existing UVM environment is the canonical adoption path: a PSS DSL script compiles to a UVM test, and the user can iterate on the PSS source without recompiling the simulation image [Bridge the Portable Test and Stimulus to UVM Simulation Environment, 2018]. For controller-based subsystems, PSS even opens a path to *reusing firmware* as the realization layer, since the abstract scenario can describe a firmware-driven test rather than a UVM one [A Novel Approach to Reuse Firmware, 2021].

## Pitfalls, adoption, and the realization-layer tax

Adopting PSS is not free, and the early-adoption papers are candid about the costs. Building and validating PSS models is time-intensive, and qualified models often aren't available early in the project, which is why teams have reached for **virtual prototyping** to validate PSS models before RTL or an emulator exists [Adopting Accellera's Portable Stimulus Standard, 2021]. The realization layer is a recurring tax: every target needs its own APIs, and the integration between the PSS model and its environment must be designed deliberately to maximize reuse of both the environment and the PSS description [Maximize PSS Reuse with Unified Test Realization Layer, 2021]. Layering PSS on top of an existing UVM block-level testbench is a real engineering decision with five concrete challenges — compile-time parameters, run-time configuration, inheritance, partial configuration, and semantics — that should be weighed before committing [Does It Pay Off To Add Portable Stimulus Layer On Top Of UVM?, 2020].

The strategic question is also real. Multiple PSS-flavored technologies existed before the standard, and betting early can lock you into a proprietary flow that evaporates; betting late means missing the automation and the chance to shape the standard [PSS: The Promises and Pitfalls of Early Adoption, 2019]. The trend across the corpus is that PSS is settling into its sweet spots — system-level stress, coherency, deadlock, multi-platform signoff, post-silicon reuse — while teams get smarter about *not* forcing it into places where a good UVM sequence already does the job. The recent push to extend one model across DV, silicon validation, and **ATE** for device signoff is the strongest argument yet that the portability bet is finally paying off [Accelerating Device Sign-off, 2025][There and Back Again, 2026].
