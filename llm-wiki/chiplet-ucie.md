# Chiplet and Multi-Die (UCIe) Verification

> *Moore's Law ran into a reticle limit and a yield wall, and the industry's answer was to stop trying to print one giant die and start gluing several smaller ones into a single package. That is the chiplet story, and **UCIe** — the Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express — is the open standard promising that die-to-die links will be as boring and reliable as PCIe. Boring, of course, is exactly what verification has to guarantee, and nothing about verifying a multi-die system is boring. You are now booting four chips at once over links that did not exist in the monolithic world, proving coherency across die boundaries, and running workloads that take 300 hours on a single testbench. The DVCon chiplet corpus is essentially year one of a brand new subfield, written down in real time. Let's dig in.*

## What changed when the chip became chips

A multi-die system integrates **pre-verified IPs, subsystems, and partially verified chiplets** over an on-package interconnect, because monolithic SoCs for generative AI and hyperscalers have grown too large to manufacture economically [Breaking barriers in Advanced Multi-Chiplet AI SoCs, 2025]. **UCIe** is the open, multi-protocol, high-bandwidth (up to 64 GT/s per lane), low-latency die-to-die standard that makes this practical, and its emergence is widely framed as a paradigm shift: pre-verified off-the-shelf components in different technology nodes are assembled into complex systems far faster than a monolithic redesign [Beyond Boundaries, 2024]. The cost is a brand new verification surface — the die-to-die (D2D) interface itself, plus every system-level behavior that crosses it.

The structural shift is that a chip is no longer one RTL hierarchy. A server SoC may be split into a compute die and a base die, manufactured separately and joined through advanced packaging, and there is no mature ecosystem of EDA tools, standards, or methods for creating and managing the disaggregated interconnects between them [Disaggregated methodology in Multi-die SoC, 2023]. Early architecture exploration is itself a research problem, since multi-die choices (what to disaggregate, how to partition IPs, how to budget power and communication) have to be made long before RTL exists [Early Architecture Exploration Of Multi Die Designs, 2024]. Even the PHY is more complex: the UCIe multi-module PHY scales bandwidth by ganging modules, and verifying it requires handling degraded-lane configurations and mainband data integrity in both transmit and receive directions [Navigating the Maze, 2024].

## How multi-die verification flows are built

The single biggest tooling shift is the move to **distributed and n-die simulation**. Because end-to-end multi-chiplet scenarios — boot, UCIe link bring-up, inter-chiplet transfer, NPU AI workload distribution — routinely hit 300+ hour runtimes and extreme memory utilization, teams reuse a single-chiplet testbench as a building block and run several synchronized instances across an LSF farm, communicating only at user-specified asynchronous intervals rather than in lockstep [Breaking barriers, 2025]. Tuning that asynchronous delay to an optimum (around 50 ps in one reported case) dramatically cuts runtime by avoiding per-event synchronization. A complementary **multi-die co-simulation framework** uses parallel-computing techniques to run dies concurrently and reduce overall simulation time for HPC-class systems [Efficient Verification of Multi-Die Systems, 2025].

Boot is a flow unto itself. A multi-chiplet secure boot has staged loaders (FBOOT, SBOOT, TBOOT), each verifying the integrity of the next, with DRAM initialization, low-speed I/O setup for boot-code transfer to secondary chiplets, and PCIe/UCIe link initialization between host, primary chiplet, and secondary chiplets — all of which has to be verified before silicon [Breaking barriers, 2025]. Dedicated distributed-simulation methodologies target this boot flow specifically, exercising the BootROM's AES/SHA authentication and personalized key management as it brings every die to a known state [Novel Approach for Verification of Multi Die Booting, 2025]. Coherency is the other flagship flow: an **adaptive VIP subsystem** stitches together UCIe, CXS, and CHI VIPs with adapter layers so that the testbench can drive a coherent view of memory across multiple chiplets and catch stale-data and race-condition bugs [Expedite multi-die coherency verification, 2025]. Multi-protocol coverage is handled by a dynamic "protocol environment" that lets one DUT be exercised under multiple UCIe configurations [Protocol Environment, 2024].

## Where the field is heading

The chiplet subfield is young enough that the open problems are still being enumerated. **Disaggregation** itself is unsolved at the methodology level: there are no standard ways to create, optimize, edit, and manage interconnects for disaggregated systems, and today's flows tolerate manual stitching only because the interconnect counts are still manageable [Disaggregated methodology in Multi-die SoC, 2023]. On the adaptive-compute side, heterogeneous SoCs (CPU + DSP + NoC + memory + I/O on reconfigurable fabric) require disaggregating IPs by workload demand, communication need, and power budget, with a scalable **Network-on-Chip** topology as the spine [Data-Driven Design for Adaptive Multi-Die SoC, 2024]. Test escape is a growing concern too: as dies multiply, the ATE flow has to handle complicated multi-die chip testing in reasonable time [Early Architecture Exploration, 2024].

The clear trajectory is that chiplet verification will absorb and extend everything the SoC-integration world already does — distributed testbenches, PSS-driven scenario generation, formal connectivity, VIP-based coherency flows — and add a die-boundary layer on top. The teams writing these papers are, in effect, defining what "chiplet signoff" will mean five years from now, and the early answer is: scalable testbenches, distributed execution, adaptive VIPs for coherency, and a serious investment in boot and PHY verification that did not exist in the monolithic era.
