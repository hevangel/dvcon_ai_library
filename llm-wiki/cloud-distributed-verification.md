# Cloud and Distributed Verification Farms

> *"Picture a verification farm at 2am: ten thousand simulator cores chewing through a nightly regression, a Jenkins dashboard turning red somewhere, and a triage bot summarizing failure logs. The whole point of a SoC verification effort is to throw compute at the problem until the bugs come out — and for the last decade that compute has been quietly migrating from on-prem racks to the cloud. Theta Yang was prototyping distributed UVM at AMD back in 2016, slicing an SoC testbench into IP-level nodes that traded TLM messages over IPC. By 2025 a Samsung team was reporting 10x faster testbench bring-up and 20-30% better simulation time from a 'disruptive distributed simulation' that let chiplets on independent servers talk over sockets. And now there's a new line item on the cloud bill nobody had a year ago: LLM tokens. So what does a modern distributed verification farm actually look like under the hood? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

A **distributed verification farm** is the compute substrate that runs regressions — a pool of simulator cores, an HPC scheduler, EDA license servers, and the storage that holds snapshots, logs, and coverage. The defining property of the modern farm is that it no longer has to live in your building. Liu Wei and colleagues at SDTECH describe the contemporary SoC CI/CD system as deliberately fusing "modern continuous integration practices in software development and the high-performance computing (HPC) environments and existing regression scripts commonly used in chip design" [Continuous Integration in SoC Design: Challenges and Solutions, 2025]. The same regression scripts that ran on a local LSF farm now run on a scheduler that can burst into the cloud when nightly demand spikes.

The interesting architectural move is **distributed simulation** — splitting a single logical testbench across multiple cooperating simulator processes. Yang's 2016 work divides "the whole SOC level testbench into multiple IP-level testbenches connected with inter process agents called IPC adapter," so each node runs a real IP-level environment and the nodes exchange internode TLM messages through inter-process communication [Distributed Simulation of UVM Testbench, 2016]. The pay-off is that the IP-level simulation environments built by block teams are reused wholesale at SoC level, with no big-bang integration testbench. Almost a decade later Gummadidala et al. push the same idea to the multi-die limit: their "Distributed Sim technology allows multiple independent simulations across homogeneous or heterogeneous designs to communicate with each other mimicking the behavior of a single unified simulation," with chiplets on separate servers talking over shared-memory sockets [Novel Approach for Verification of Multi Die Booting Using Disruptive Distributed Simulation Methodology, 2025].

## How it's used in practice

The most quoted wins come from the multi-die world, where a single monolithic testbench simply does not scale. Gummadidala et al. catalog the pain — a single-wrapper multi-die simulation means linear testbench growth, machines that can't build the database, two-week debug loops — and report that distributed simulation delivers "a 10x improvement in test bench development, 20-30 % improvement in simulation time, 15 % improvement in processor usage and 10x reduction in resource requirement over a conventional single wrapper approach" [Novel Approach for Verification of Multi Die Booting..., 2025]. Kothari et al. echo the same shift for advanced multi-chiplet AI SoCs, where emulation and boot verification have to absorb pre-verified IP, sub-systems, and partially verified chiplets across UCIe links [Breaking barriers in Advanced Multi-Chiplet AI SoCs using scalable UCIe and Boot Verification and Emulation techniques, 2025]. Hybrid testbench-and-software flows on emulation are the natural extension — Bhattacharya and Khan describe running "mixed software application based and testbench based profiles" for cloud-scale software-defined devices [Hybrid Approach to Testbench and Software Driven Verification on Emulation, 2018].

The newest member of the farm stack is the **LLM assistant**, and it has its own cost curve. Jose points out that continuous regressions generate tens of thousands of logs, and "each LLM call, however small, accumulates into non-trivial cloud bills or competes with on-prem GPU capacity," yet traditional EDA infrastructure has no native notion of "LLM cost" [Passive Token Accounting For Cost-Aware LLM Assistance In Continuous SoC Verification Workflows, 2026]. His Passive Token Accounting (PTA) proposal — part of the dVI platform — is essentially the cloud-billing layer verification teams are going to need once the triage bot is reading every log.

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The first pitfall is performance theater: a farm with more cores isn't faster if the testbench is single-threaded. Cummings, Rose and Sherer's perennial "Yikes! Why is My SystemVerilog Still So Slooooow?" reminds us that simulators got faster between 2012 and 2019, but "the question engineers raise is often the same" — UVM, randomization, and assertions still dominate runtime, and no amount of cloud capacity fixes a solver-bound test [Yikes! Why is My SystemVerilog Still So Slooooow?, 2019]. The second pitfall is **distributed-debug fidelity**: Yang notes that a two-node prototype needs its IPC adapter implemented carefully in SystemC to preserve TLM semantics, and the multi-die work goes to lengths to keep "waves, coverage and logs remain same as single die simulation" so engineers aren't debugging a distributed artifact [Distributed Simulation of UVM Testbench, 2016; Novel Approach..., 2025]. The third is **license economics**: burst capacity is useless if the solver license pool is the real bottleneck, a tension that drives the regression-selection and farm-management work cross-linked below.

The field is clearly heading toward farms that are simultaneously simulation, emulation, and inference clusters — with LLM triage as a first-class, metered workload. Hierarchical and hybrid data models for CDC analysis [Fully Hierarchical CDC Analysis Using Comprehensive CDC Meta Database, 2019; Comprehensive IP to SoC Clock Domain Crossing Verification Using Hybrid Data Model, 2018] show the same instinct of pushing analysis itself into distributed, IP-aware passes that fit on a farm rather than one giant flat run. Expect the cloud bill of the future to have three line items — simulator hours, emulator hours, and LLM tokens — and the verification lead to need all three on one dashboard.
