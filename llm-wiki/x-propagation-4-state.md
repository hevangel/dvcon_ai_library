# X-Propagation and 4-State Verification

> *"SystemVerilog has four logic values — 0, 1, Z, and X — and the X is the one nobody trusts. It is supposed to mean 'unknown,' the simulator's honest confession that it has no idea what bit should be there. The problem is that Verilog's evaluation rules let conditional statements quietly resolve an X to a definite 0 or 1 at RTL, hiding the very bugs that gate-level simulation (GLS) would later expose at the netlist. Stuart Sutherland titled his 2013 DVCon paper 'I'm Still In Love With My X!' precisely because that fourth state is the only thing standing between a green regression and a chip that ships with a masked-off reset bug. Add power-aware simulation, where UPF injects fresh Xs from every power-down domain, and the picture gets worse — Durgesh Prasad and Jitesh Bansal showed that the standard X-propagation techniques 'lack the knowledge of power intent' and drown engineers in noise. So how do you verify that unknowns get caught, not propagated to the output? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**X-optimism** is the behavior where RTL simulation resolves an unknown input to a known output, masking bugs that real silicon would surface. The classic locus is the conditional statement: an `if (sel)` where `sel` is X picks the `else` branch in simulation, but a real gate may do something else entirely. **X-pessimism** is the opposite — the simulator propagates X aggressively, surfacing failures that the silicon would never produce. Sutherland's survey argues both viewpoints are partially correct and that "the tables have turned" on the 2-state promise of the late 2000s: vendors who once pitched 2-state simulation as faster and sufficient now ship X-propagation-aware modes that recover the safety of 4-state [I'm Still In Love With My X!, 2013].

The modern answer to X-optimism is **X-propagation simulation**: a proprietary mode (VCS, and now Cadence/Siemens equivalents) that rewrites conditional semantics so X inputs produce X outputs the way real gates would. Baddam and Sukhija document the Verilog implementation and then port it to VHDL, showing where the LRM gets in the way — VHDL data operations like `+` are "not X-propagation aware" and produce all-X results even when only one operand is unknown [Challenges of VHDL X-propagation Simulations, 2015]. They petitioned the IEEE P1076 VASG to add realistic X-propagation semantics to the LRM so tool vendors would model X consistently.

## How it's used in practice

In low-power flows, the X problem gets harder, not easier. UPF-based power-aware simulators deliberately corrupt every flop and combinational input in a power-down domain to X, then propagate X across isolation cells and into always-on regions. Prasad and Bansal showed that running plain X-propagation on top of this injects false alarms because the simulator has "no knowledge of power intent" [Is your Power Aware design really x-aware ?, 2014]. Their fix is a power-aware X technique that knows which Xs are legitimate corruption and which are real bugs. George, Kumar, and Shim take this further: instead of waiting for the scoreboard to catch a data-integrity failure, their flow "translates the power design into checkers with assertions" that flag X-propagation and isolation violations at the source, cutting waveform-tracing turnaround time [Automation for Early Detection of Xpropagation in Power-Aware Simulation Verification using UPF IEEE 1801, 2023]. The same logic now extends to emulation — Khondkar and Budlong describe the "industry's first 4 state logic based hardware emulation technology" for UPF corruption models, which is the only way to keep X semantics honest when test runs move onto an FPGA fabric [Complexities & Challenges of UPF Corruption Model in Low Power Emulation, 2024].

On the formal side, Zhao, Yan, and Feng at Broadcom argue you cannot rely on GLS to find every X-optimism bug and propose a formal X-propagation app: the tool reads RTL, automatically asserts that no X reaches clocks, resets, control signals, or output ports, and reports any formally reachable X to the user as a candidate bug [Practical Approach Using a Formal App to Detect X-Optimism-Related RTL Bugs, 2014]. Piper and Vimjam give the broader taxonomy — structural analysis, formal analysis, coding for X-accuracy, and random seeding of state initial values — and stress that solving X up front also unblocks power analysis, which otherwise has to wait for a clean netlist [X-Propagation Woes: Masking Bugs at RTL and Unnecessary Debug at the Netlist, 2012].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The dominant pitfall is **lockup debugging**. X-propagation runs frequently produce a huge list of X-endowed signals at end-of-test, and Baddam and Sukhija describe their workaround: capture reference waves from the non-X run, prune signals whose reference value is also X, prune signals with no events, and only then root-cause the remainder — because "lock up failures in X-propagation simulations are difficult to debug" [Challenges of VHDL X-propagation Simulations, 2015]. A second pitfall is convergence: when do you stop? The pragmatic answer is "rerun all sign-off tests with X-propagation options and match normal coverage and pass rate," but that breaks the moment your sign-off regression lives on emulation or FPGA prototyping, which historically could not model X. The 2024 Cadence 4-state emulator is one answer; another is gated RTL X-propagation runs done before GLS, since GLS itself also serves synthesis/timing/power purposes and is too expensive to be the only X net.

The field is converging on a layered strategy: 2-state for speed where the design is well-exercised, X-propagation on sign-off RTL regressions, formal X-propagation apps on critical control blocks, and 4-state power-aware UPF simulation for low-power sign-off [Is your Power Aware design really x-aware ?, 2014]. Reset schemes are a particularly rich target — Liu, Yang, and Levitt show that "simulation alone cannot efficiently verify all reset scenarios" and recommend formal coverage of reset sources that often manifest as X at the outputs of uninitialized flops [Using Formal Techniques to Verify System on Chip Reset Schemes, 2013]. Decker's earlier case study makes the cultural point that low-power verification "depends on positive pessimism" — teams that embrace X-pessimism ship cleaner silicon than those who treat X as noise to silence [Case Study: Low-Power Verification Success Depends on Positive Pessimism, 2011].
