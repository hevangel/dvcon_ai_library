# DVFS — Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling Verification

> *"Energy is proportional to voltage squared. That single equation, more than any marketing slide, explains why every modern SoC from a phone to a data-center die ships with DVFS — drop the voltage a little when the workload lets you, and you buy back a lot of battery or HVAC. But voltage and frequency can't move independently: the maximum frequency a domain can hit is set by its voltage, and if you drop voltage before you drop frequency — or raise frequency before you raise voltage — the logic stops meeting setup and the chip silently corrupts. Khan was writing about mixed-signal DVFS verification back in 2010; Samsung's Dalal and colleagues are still publishing on Clock Tree Network verification under DVFS in 2025. Why so much staying power? Because every node adds more voltage rails, more operating points, and more domains that have to transition in lockstep. Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS)** is the technique of scaling a domain's operating voltage and clock frequency in real time based on workload, with the goal of running at the lowest voltage-and-frequency pair that still meets the application's needs [Mixed Signal Verification of Dynamic Adaptive Power Management in Low Power SoC, 2010]. The set of legal voltage-frequency pairs a domain can operate at are its **Operating Performance Points (OPPs)**, and verifying DVFS means proving the chip throttles, boosts, and survives every legal transition between them — power-up, power-down, voltage step, frequency step, and every interleaving of those events.

The defining difficulty is that DVFS sits at the analog/digital seam. Voltage scaling is done by external regulators — often Low-Dropout Regulators (LDOs) — that UPF still has no native way to model, leaving verification engineers to hand-write SystemVerilog behavior for each one [Low Power Verification With LDO, 2016]. Frequency scaling flows through a clock tree whose dividers, muxes, and gating cells have to reconfigure cleanly while the rest of the chip keeps running. The Clock Tree Network (CTN) that distributes the resulting clock is, in Samsung's words, "one of the complex design structures in SOC," comprising numerous dynamically controlled clock components to establish DVFS and hardware-controlled auto clock gating schemes [Robust Verification of Clock Tree Network using "Clock Monitor" Integrated by ACRMG, 2025]. Handover et al. make the wider point: traditional low-power verification only checks the power-control logic itself, but DVFS changes the *clocking* of the design — and that has to be verified too [Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015].

## How it's used in practice

Two patterns dominate. The first is **digital-centric mixed-signal (DMS) verification**, where the analog regulator and the digital DVFS controller are verified together using Real Number Models for speed, with a small subset of critical tests re-run against SPICE/Verilog-A for accuracy. Khan's 2010 methodology runs the full plan at RNM speed, then swaps in analog models for the targeted tests with no testbench changes — the RNM and analog models are pin-compatible [Mixed Signal Verification of Dynamic Adaptive Power Management in Low Power SoC, 2010]. The same DMS philosophy shows up in PMIC verification, where a UVM-MS environment drives power gating, voltage changing, and frequency scaling against a mixed-signal IC, modeling each interface by its dominant electrical property [With great power comes great responsibility: A method to verify PMICs using UVM-MS, 2014].

The second pattern is **clock-tree-centric verification with custom monitors**. Dalal et al. built a Clock Monitor (CLKMON) module that observes, analyzes, and validates the dynamic behavior of the CTN under DVFS — covering whether every IP clock frequency in every voltage-rail and DVFS-level scenario lands where it should, using SOC-level DVFS covergroups [Robust Verification of Clock Tree Network using "Clock Monitor" Integrated by ACRMG, 2025]. An earlier paper from the same team automated the clock reference model (CRM) generation with an "Accelerated Clock Reference Model Generator" (ACRMG), so the expected-clock oracle for every clock component can be regenerated as the design evolves [Quality Driven Analysis of Clock Tree Network using "Accelerated Clock Reference Model Generator", 2024]. At the IP level, Kim et al. verify dynamic IP clock frequency on the SOC with an auto-generated "Active Clock Monitor" bound through a YAML clock-tree database, cutting simulation time by 66% and debugging points by up to 75% by replacing waveform dumps with log-based analysis [An Efficient Method to Verify Dynamic IP Clock Frequency on SOC Level by Using Auto-generated Active Clock Monitor, 2021]. And on the power side, Khondkar shows how to **probe UPF dynamic objects** — supply-set states, supply-port states, logic-port conditions, isolation/retention strategy states — to build a custom low-power verification platform that tracks the discrete voltage changes DVFS makes in real time [Probing UPF Dynamic Objects: Methodologies to Build Your Custom Low-Power Verification Platform, 2020].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The headline pitfall is that **voltage domains multiply CDC complexity**. When DVFS is in play, a single clock design can fragment into multiple asynchronous clocks and thousands of voltage-domain-crossing (VDC) paths; Handover et al. report one CPU core that looked like one clock until voltage domains were accounted for, at which point it had six asynchronous clocks and 4,893 VDC paths [Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015]. Isolation and retention cells — which DVFS-rich designs rely on heavily — introduce their own CDC hazards: an isolation enable from an async domain can violate setup-hold at the destination, and the assumption that save/restore protects retention logic from metastability turns out to be false under DVFS [Power Aware CDC Verification of Dynamic Frequency and Voltage Scaling (DVFS) Artifacts, 2015]. The broader lesson, echoed by Nawal and Jain, is that next-gen low-power SoCs stack so many techniques — fine-grained clock gating, multi-domain power shutoff, body biasing, DVFS — that the verification scope explodes at RTL and the timing implications ripple all the way to gate level [Complex Low Power Verification Challenges in NextGen SoCs: Taming the Beast!, 2014].

The field is heading toward **coverage-driven, RTL-first DVFS verification** and toward **emulation for realistic scenarios**. Bhargava et al. tackle the coverage gap directly, asking what it takes to close power-aware verification when ifdef macros and power-aware models create multiple RTL flavors — covering all power objects, all power sequences, and all interactions between power and functional modes [Unveil the Mystery of Code Coverage in Low-Power Designs: Achieving Power Aware Verification Closure, 2018]. The clock-tree team at Samsung has converged on automating the entire verification oracle: CRM generation, CLKMON monitoring, and DVFS coverage definition all driven from the same machine-readable clock-tree description, so verifying DVFS stops being a hand-built per-project exercise [Quality Driven Analysis of Clock Tree Network using "Accelerated Clock Reference Model Generator", 2024]. The long arc is clear: as voltage rails and OPP counts keep growing, the only way to verify DVFS in reasonable time is to push the analysis to RTL, automate the monitor and oracle generation, and treat DVFS coverage as a first-class signoff metric — not a special case buried inside low-power verification.
