# Regression Management and Infrastructure

> *"Every verification team's morning starts the same way: coffee, then the regression dashboard. A nightly run of tens of thousands of simulations came back overnight across a farm of LSF-managed cores, and now you're staring at a wall of green, a scatter of red, and the question that decides the day — did we move forward or backward? DVCon's regression papers are, underneath the tooling, a fifteen-year argument about how to make that dashboard tell the truth cheaply. The early fight was pure throughput: how do you keep a compute farm busy when multi-core simulations don't scale linearly and LSF has a habit of cycle-stealing your threads? The modern fight is orchestration: when a run finishes, who (or what) decides what to run next? The answer is becoming 'an AI agent,' which would have sounded insane in 2011 and sounds inevitable in 2026. So what does a regression infrastructure actually need to do, and why did it grow into the most-instrumented part of the flow? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

A **regression** is the automated, scheduled re-running of a project's entire verification suite against the evolving RTL — nightly at minimum, increasingly on every commit. The infrastructure has three layers: a **compute farm** (LSF, SGE, or a cloud burst), a **job scheduler** that dispatches tests onto cores, and a **metrics/reporting layer** that merges results and coverage into something a human (or lately an agent) can read. Sharma et al. lay out the canonical picture: "CAD teams … create Regression Infrastructures across Load Sharing Facility (LSF) setups and compute farms to use the multiple cores per machines optimally," and the hard part is that "multi-core simulations throw a different set of challenges" because "the gains do not scale in the same order as the numbers of cores" [Parallel Computing for Functional Verification and Compute Farms, 2011]. Throughput on a farm is a scheduling problem before it is a simulation problem.

Above the scheduler sits the **metrics layer**. Crutchfield et al. describe the Cypress VMS: "a centralized, web-based, verification monitoring and reporting system" that "gathers metrics across projects company-wide and make[s] them available by anyone on demand," polling LSF for "max active jobs, max pending jobs, pending reason and wait time, and load statistics," tying the data to project revisions via IC Manage, and rolling it into a per-regression UCDB (Unified Coverage DataBase) [Regressions in the 21 st Century -Tools for Global Surveillance, 2016]. The dashboard is not a nice-to-have; it is the artifact the whole flow is judged by.

## How it's used in practice

The first practical preoccupation is **farm economics**. Hansson and Granath are blunt: "companies spend more and more money on computer farms, yet there is never enough computer resources," "if additional computer resources are put in place they are consumed almost instantly," and "it is very hard to do a cost-benefit analysis on com[pute] resources" [Optimal Usage of the Computer Farm for Regression Testing, 2016]. Lacey and Powell take the engineering side, "explor[ing] the various requirements that impact the optimal construction of a regression farm" — hardware cost, license cost, use models — and how to "continuously track the performance … to ensure the optimization is maintained" [Creating the Optimal Regression Farm Infrastructure, 2018]. Sharma et al.'s answer is the **middleware**: a learn-and-apply LSF wrapper that biases scheduling between serial and multi-core jobs based on historical runtime and memory data, dodging "cycle stealing," paging, and cross-queue collisions [Parallel Computing for Functional Verification and Compute Farms, 2011].

The second preoccupation is **continuous integration at RTL scale**. Thoo and Burgoon's AMD "Autocuration" is the prototype: "a dynamic process to determine the appropriate subset of regression test suites that need to be run for each source commit," validating every change to the version-controlled repository of a next-generation microprocessor core [Autocuration, 2012]. Crutchfield and Ellis generalize the lesson — that project-borrowed scripts don't scale and you need a real verification-management environment built once and reused [Bringing Regression Systems into the 21 st Century, 2014] — and Meyer shows the metric-collection side on a complex ARM-based SoC with distributed hierarchical caches, where "understanding how well the components are verified is a challenge" because "the data is spread across one or more regressions" [So you think you have good stimulus, 2014]. Runtime tricks matter too: Vanshlata et al.'s vManager Save & Restart "significantly reduce[s] simulation times, improve[s] license utilization, increas[es] debug efficiency" [Accelerating SoC Verification Signoff, 2021], and Besyakov's SVDB Gateway offloads verification data to SQLite via DPI-C to "reduce memory footprint, enhance compilation speed, and improve simulation throughput" as designs scale past a billion gates [Database Driven RTL Simulation, 2026].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The pitfall the field keeps rediscovering is that **a regression run produces more signal than any human can act on**. Noh et al. name it precisely: regression pipelines "remain largely linear and execution-centric: failure and coverage signals are often siloed, so a human manager must interpret and relay them instead of having them directly drive the next triage and follow-up decisions," which "increases turnaround time and creates persistent bottlenecks when designs or verification environments change" [Multi-Agent Orchestration for Autonomous Regression Management, 2026]. Lacey and Powell's farm-optimization caveat is the infrastructural twin: without continuous tracking, the "optimal" farm drifts out of tune [Creating the Optimal Regression Farm Infrastructure, 2018].

Where this is heading is **agentic, closed-loop regression management**. Choi et al.'s "3-tiered agentic AI regression framework" structures the run into user, block, and central tiers "each aligned with a distinct verification s[cope]," killing the "monolithic regression systems [that] repeatedly execute massive test sets without considering hierarchical design boundaries" [A 3-tiered agentic AI Framework for Verification Regression, 2026]. Fang's Etabot is a "local-first, multi-agent framework that automates verification management end to end — from running regressions and merging a unified coverage database to triaging failures, collecting midpoint metrics, forecasting finish dates, and producing manager-ready status," all reachable from a CLI and an MCP-enabled chatbot [Etabot, 2026]. Bhattacharjee even brings in Hyperledger blockchain to "facilitate the automation of regression management … resolve disk space-related issues and also enable collaboration of more engineers" [Integrating RTL design and UVM Testbench with Hyperledger, 2026]. Connectivity and coherency regressions feed the same machine — Ikram et al.'s weekly formal connectivity suite [2019] and Jang et al.'s PSS-based coherency/deadlock flows [2019] are stimulus sources the farm has to schedule. The regression farm stopped being a batch queue a few years ago; it is becoming an autonomous system that runs, reads, and re-plans itself.
