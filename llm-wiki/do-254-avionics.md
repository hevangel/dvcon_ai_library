# DO-254 — Airborne Electronic Hardware

> *"Nobody wants to be the engineer whose FPGA crashed an aircraft. That fear, codified, is DO-254. RTCA's 'Design Assurance Guidance for Airborne Electronic Hardware' ranks every piece of avionics logic on five levels — DAL A through DAL E — where DAL A means 'failure could cause catastrophic, irreversible results,' up to and including the aircraft crashing with many deaths aboard. The catch is that DO-254 is a guideline, not a checklist: it tells you to demonstrate assurance, then leaves the verification artifacts to you and your DER (Designated Engineering Representative). DVCon has quietly become the place where avionics teams — Rockwell Collins, ASELSAN, Patmos Engineering, Aldec — compare notes on what those artifacts actually look like in practice. The recurring surprise? Requirement-based verification is the floor, not the ceiling, and for DAL A/B you owe the regulator a pile of robustness tests, advanced verification, and independence that most consumer-chip teams never even think about. So what does a DO-254 flow really demand, and where does it bend? Let's dig in."*

## What it is

**DO-254** (and its European twin ED-80) is the safety-critical guideline for airborne electronic hardware — programmable logic, ASICs, and custom ICs in aircraft. It defines five **Design Assurance Levels (DAL A-E)**, with criticality decreasing from A to E; in a DAL A system, "failure of the hardware would prevent the aircraft to continue flying safely," so the verification burden is maximal [Types of Robustness Test According to DO-254 Guideline for Avionic Systems, 2022]. The backbone of compliance is **requirements-based verification (RBV)** — every requirement traces down to a verification case, a procedure, and a result, in a strict waterfall where "the requirements must be developed before the design and verification begins" and "the relationship between these documents must be traceable" [Do You Know What Your Assertions Are Up To?, 2016].

What makes DO-254 harder than ordinary verification is the **independence** and **completeness** demands layered on top of RBV. For DAL A and B, the testbench must be "independently developed, reviewed or executed," and verification has to satisfy the elemental analysis defined in DO-254 appendix B — functional and code coverage metrics on the design source [Deploying HLS in a DO-254/ED-80 Workflow, 2020]. Functional simulation alone "cannot provide designers with the verification completeness criteria," so the standard "requires that safety- and mission-critical designs be verified on the real hardware for certification purposes" — FPGA-based validation in the loop is not optional [FPGA-based Clock Domain Crossing Validation for Safety-Critical Designs, 2019].

## How it's used in practice

The workhorse artifact is the **robustness test suite**. For DAL A/B PLDs, Aselsan's KILINÇ, AKSU and BAYSAL describe a taxonomy that pushes inputs to and beyond their specified limits: boundary and duty-cycle tests at the legal-edge tolerances, reset robustness tests that yank the system mid-operation, glitch-filter tests that inject unwanted pulses to confirm the filter protects meaningful data, and invalid-state-transition tests that fire an FSM into unexpected states and check recovery [Types of Robustness Test According to DO-254 Guideline, 2022]. These are fault-injection-adjacent — "performed actively in PLDs of DAL A and DAL B systems," driving the hardware with conditions "at and beyond the given limits by requirements" to find the latent instabilities [Types of Robustness Test According to DO-254 Guideline, 2022].

**Assertion-based verification and VIP** carry the traceability story. Rockwell Collins' Lee Smith introduced augmented VIP that modifies how assertions are tracked and recorded, so a DER can see evidence that an assertion was actually exercised even when it did not fire — the gap that ordinary coverage misses [Do You Know What Your Assertions Are Up To?, 2016]. But VIPs in a DO-254 flow have to be **qualified** themselves: Cerisier, Carmagnat and Basili point out that safety standards demand the verification environment be qualified, so a VIP that silently lets bugs through is itself a certification risk, and constrained-random generation has to be reconciled with requirement traceability [Qualification of a Verification IP under Requirement Based Verification standards, 2018]. Efody maps exactly where coverage-driven/constrained-random verification and requirement-based verification collide and how to reconcile them across both ISO 26262 and DO-254 flows [Who takes the driver seat for ISO 26262 and DO 254 verification?, 2015], and Chapman, Galpin and Bart frame requirements-driven verification as the standards-compliant variant of metric-driven verification [Requirements driven Verification methodology (for standards compliance), 2014].

**Hardware-in-the-loop and CDC** are the recurring technical themes. Gnusin argues that complex FPGA CDC verification must move onto real hardware for DO-254 signoff, because the simulator only covers a limited test-vector subset [FPGA-based Clock Domain Crossing Validation for Safety-Critical Designs, 2019]. And as newer tooling enters the flow, the question becomes *can it be trusted enough* — Reeve, Wiltgen and Brinson describe the requirements and considerations for deploying High-Level Synthesis inside a DO-254 workflow, where the SystemC/C++ source becomes the design of record, an HLS testbench collects the appendix-B elemental coverage, and the generated RTL inherits verification credit only "if appropriate tool assessment measures are taken" [Deploying HLS in a DO-254/ED-80 Workflow, 2020].

## Pitfalls and where the field is heading

The central pitfall is **traceability at scale**. Every requirement, verification case, procedure, and result has to stay linked across a waterfall that resists iteration, and a single broken trace can stall certification. Augmented VIP and ABV help, but only if assertions are demonstrably exercised — Smith's core insight is that an assertion that never fires is worthless evidence unless you can prove it was hit [Do You Know What Your Assertions Are Up To?, 2016]. The second pitfall is **tool qualification**: any tool — HLS, a VIP, a simulator — entering a DO-254 flow has to demonstrate "sufficient safeness to use," and that evidence is rarely off-the-shelf [Deploying HLS in a DO-254/ED-80 Workflow, 2020] [Qualification of a Verification IP under Requirement Based Verification standards, 2018]. Robustness testing is where most teams underestimate: the categories are simple to name but laborious to engineer, with inputs "pushed to their minimum or maximum values" to find the latent failures [Types of Robustness Test According to DO-254 Guideline, 2022].

The field is heading toward **borrowed rigor from adjacent safety domains and modern tooling**. DO-254 and ISO 26262 share so much methodological DNA — requirements traceability, advanced verification for the top levels, qualified tooling — that teams now explicitly reconcile CD/CR with RBV once and reuse the mapping across both standards [Who takes the driver seat for ISO 26262 and DO 254 verification?, 2015]. HLS is creeping into certified flows because it shifts verification earlier and yields appendix-B coverage artifacts "sooner," reusing the working HLS verification environment for the post-HLS RTL [Deploying HLS in a DO-254/ED-80 Workflow, 2020]. And lessons from formal qualifier workflows — Scaria, Sood and Seligman's approach to qualifying assertion-based VIPs through formal plus fault injection — are increasingly imported to give DERs harder evidence than directed tests ever could [Let's be Formal While Talking About Verification Quality, 2018]. The guideline hasn't changed much; the verification behind it has.
